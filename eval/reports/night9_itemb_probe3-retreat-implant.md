## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

Based on Level I and Level IIIa evidence, this decision is not a foregone conclusion in favour of extraction: reported survival of single implants and retained endodontically-treated teeth (including retreated and apicoected molars) is broadly comparable over the first 5–10 years, and no direct comparative trials exist [[PMID:28343928]] [[PMID:25306305]]. After a failed non-surgical retreatment on a *restorable* molar, apical microsurgery is the next endodontic step and yields ~86–94% healed cases at 1 year and ~75–82% at 5–10 years when performed with a microscope and an MTA root-end filling [[PMID:20951283]] [[PMID:22515881]] [[PMID:30827766]]. Extraction and implant placement is the correct choice when the tooth is structurally compromised (vertical root fracture, unrestorable, deep apico-marginal defect, hopeless periodontal support) or when apical surgery is contraindicated (anatomical, patient, or case-complexity reasons per the AAE Case Difficulty guidelines [AAE — Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form and Guidelines (2022; current; US)]); an important caveat is that dental implants have been shown to significantly increase the risk of adjacent tooth loss, chiefly from root fracture [[PMID:39154079]].

Decision framework, grounded in the retrieved evidence:
- **Restorability and remaining tooth structure** — the dominant driver. Longitudinal root fractures accounted for most late losses after apical surgery [[PMID:30827766]], and coronal restoration quality is one of the strongest predictors of both healing and survival [[PMID:24461401]] [[PMID:41062676]].
- **Endodontic prognosis of surgery** — endodontic microsurgery (EMS) is significantly superior to traditional root-end surgery (94% vs 59% pooled success) [[PMID:20951283]], and offers higher short-term success than a second non-surgical retreatment (77.8% vs 70.9% at 2–4 years), although non-surgical retreatment overtakes it at 4–6 years (83.0% vs 71.8%) [[PMID:19567310]].
- **Periodontal / bone support** — interproximal bone level >3 mm from the CEJ dropped 5-year healing from 78% to 53% after apical surgery [[PMID:22515881]].
- **Case complexity** — a second retreatment attempt in a molar with a persistent lesion, canal transportation, or a separated instrument may be lower yield than surgery; AAE difficulty assessment supports specialist referral [AAE — Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form and Guidelines (2022; current; US)].
- **Patient factors** — the patient's stated preference matters, but should be informed by the fact that apical surgery is a one-visit procedure that preserves the natural tooth with a genuinely favourable prognosis when indicated, and that adjacent-tooth complications after implant placement are non-trivial [[PMID:39154079]].

## EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Cochrane Reviews**

The 2016 Cochrane review by Del Fabbro et al. (20 RCTs) directly addresses the surgical-vs-non-surgical retreatment question but concluded there is no clear evidence of superiority for either approach at 1-year or 4–10-year follow-up (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35 at 1 year), with the caveat that the evidence base is very low quality [[PMID:27759881]]. The review does provide two findings relevant to the surgical arm if that route is chosen: MTA root-end filling healed significantly better than smoothed orthograde gutta-percha (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.14–2.24), and ultrasonic root-end preparation showed a borderline advantage over burs. Notably, the Cochrane review makes no comparison against extraction/implant — that question has never been addressed by an RCT.

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**

Chercoles-Ruiz et al. systematically reviewed 60 studies comparing endodontic treatment/retreatment/apical surgery against single-tooth implants and concluded both are valid and complementary, with implant survival higher than endodontic success in isolated comparisons, but no important between-treatment differences emerging until ≥8 years; only a Grade B recommendation was possible because no RCTs exist [[PMID:28343928]]. Torabinejad et al.'s systematic review of endodontic microsurgery vs single implants likewise found implant survival slightly higher at 4–6 years but with heterogeneous outcome criteria that precluded valid comparison [[PMID:25306305]]. Torabinejad's earlier meta-analysis of nonsurgical retreatment vs endodontic surgery found surgery superior at 2–4 years (77.8% vs 70.9%) but retreatment superior at 4–6 years (83.0% vs 71.8%), with surgical success declining over time and nonsurgical success improving — a critical temporal pattern for counselling [[PMID:19567310]]. Setzer's meta-analysis is the single most influential finding for the surgical option: endodontic microsurgery (94% pooled success) markedly outperformed traditional root-end surgery (59%), with a relative risk ratio of 1.58 [[PMID:20951283]].

**Level II — Prospective Studies**

Ng et al.'s large prospective cohort (858 secondary root canal treatments) reported 4-year tooth survival of 95.3% after retreatment — essentially identical to primary treatment — with periapical healing of 80% [[PMID:21366627]] [[PMID:21366626]]. Key prognostic factors reducing survival included preoperative pain, iatrogenic perforation, extruded root filling (long-term), and absence of a definitive cast restoration; these should inform whether a *repeat* retreatment is realistic or whether the tooth has already accumulated too many negative predictors. Von Arx et al.'s 5-year and 10-year prospective series on apical microsurgery with MTA reported 75.9% healed at 5 years and 81.5% at 10 years (with maxillary molars reaching 95.2%), and identified MTA (vs SuperEBA) and intact interproximal bone (≤3 mm from CEJ) as the two dominant positive predictors [[PMID:22515881]] [[PMID:30827766]]. Notably, many late losses in the 10-year cohort were due to longitudinal root fractures, reinforcing that structural integrity — not endodontic outcome — often decides the long-term fate.

**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**

Van Nieuwenhuysen's 25-year cohort of 2,500 treatments showed treatment success dropping from 85% at 5 years to 60% at 20 years, and tooth preservation from 90% to 50% — dominated by disease and patient factors rather than technical details [[PMID:36683563]]. Jurič et al.'s 13-year cohort (1,259 teeth) found 79.9% success under strict criteria with 11 prognostic factors, including tooth type (molars OR 0.45), preoperative PAI, defective coronal restoration, and injury history [[PMID:38243912]]. Azim et al.'s "Buffalo" microsurgery cohort reported 93% survival and 86–88% success, with major procedural error the only significant negative predictor [[PMID:32975855]]. Critically, Afrashtehfar et al.'s large cohort (15,637 teeth) demonstrated that teeth adjacent to implants had a substantially higher risk of loss (10-year survival 89.2% vs 99.3%; OR 13.15), with root fracture the leading cause — a direct counterweight to the "just extract and place an implant" reflex [[PMID:39154079]].

**Level IIIb — Case-Control**

Turan Gökduman et al. (943 teeth, ≥5-year follow-up) reported 73.5% survival/success after non-surgical retreatment, with restoration type and initial obturation quality the significant predictors; root/crown fractures accounted for two-thirds of extractions [[PMID:41062676]]. Smith et al. (1,466 teeth) showed 60-month tooth survival of 96% when retreatment was done by dentists with specialist training vs 90.5% in routine care — the odds of periapical healing were also substantially higher (OR 4.71 for moderate complexity cases), supporting referral for complex retreatment [[PMID:38512015]]. De Kuijper et al. reported cumulative survival of 91.7% at 89 months for complex endodontic retreatment of molars, with deviant canal morphology and inadequate coronal seal reducing endodontic healing [[PMID:33617944]].

**Specialty Guidelines & Position Statements**

The AAE Case Difficulty Assessment Guidelines (2022) provide the operational framework for deciding whether the general practitioner should treat, refer to a specialist, or consider extraction — a failed retreatment in a mature molar typically falls into the "high difficulty" band warranting specialist evaluation before extraction is chosen [AAE — Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form and Guidelines (2022; current; US)].

**Level V — Expert Opinion / Reviews**

Consensus expert commentary supports a tooth-first approach where the endodontic prognosis is reasonable and the tooth is restorable, with implant reserved for teeth that are structurally hopeless or where retreatment options are exhausted.

## NOT FROM THE RETRIEVED EVIDENCE BASE (standard practice, unverified against these papers)

Practical decision points that the retrieved evidence does not directly address but which inform the discussion with the patient: (1) A restorability assessment under the microscope with the existing restoration removed is essential *before* the extract-vs-retreat/surgery decision is finalised — a tooth that looks retreatable radiographically may reveal a vertical root fracture, sub-crestal caries, or inadequate ferrule at that inspection. (2) Cost, time, and biological cost differ: an implant with bone graft and definitive crown is typically a 6–12 month, multi-visit sequence, whereas apical microsurgery is generally a single visit. (3) Implant contraindications (uncontrolled diabetes, bisphosphonate/antiresorptive history, smoking, inadequate bone volume, aesthetic zone considerations) may swing the decision back toward tooth retention. (4) The patient's preference is legitimate but should be informed by realistic prognoses on both sides rather than a general perception that "implants last forever." Guidance from the AAE, ESE, and ITI positions supports individualised risk-benefit analysis rather than a default to extraction. Returning to what this evidence base does support: apical microsurgery in a restorable molar with adequate periodontal support offers ~86–94% 1-year healed rates and ~75–82% at 5–10 years [[PMID:20951283]] [[PMID:22515881]] [[PMID:30827766]], and this option should be presented to the patient before proceeding to extraction.
