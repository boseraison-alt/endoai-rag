Systematic reviews comparing endodontic microsurgery and single-tooth implants find both have high survival, with implants marginally higher at 4–6 years but with more post-treatment interventions [[PMID:25306305]] [[PMID:28343928]], and a 7-year cohort of 1,850 procedures found no significant difference in survival between endodontic retreatment and implant re-implantation once diabetes, smoking, age and arch were adjusted for [[PMID:41455543]]. Critically, teeth adjacent to implants carry a ~13-fold increased 10-year loss risk, driven mostly by root fracture [[PMID:39154079]] — this must be weighed before extracting. Extract and implant when the tooth has a vertical root fracture, unrestorable structural loss, or an identifiable non-manageable cause of failure; offer apical microsurgery (or selective root retreatment where anatomy allows) when the failure is a missed canal, an apical extraruncal biofilm, a small perforation, or inadequate obturation in an otherwise restorable tooth.\n\n---\n\n## EVIDENCE SUMMARY\n\n**Cochrane Reviews**\nThe 2016 Cochrane review of retreatment (Del Fabbro et al.) directly addressed the surgical-vs-nonsurgical retreatment question and found no clear superiority for either approach on healing at one year (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35) or at 4–10 years, though the evidence was rated very low quality; more surgical patients reported first-week pain (RR 3.34) [[PMID:27759881]]. Within surgical retreatment, MTA outperformed smoothed orthograde gutta-percha as a root-end filling (RR 1.60 for healing), and ultrasonic root-end preparation showed a borderline benefit over burs [[PMID:27759881]]. The review did not compare either endodontic option against extraction and implant, so it informs *how* to do a third attempt but not *whether* to.\n\n**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**\nTwo systematic reviews addressed the tooth-vs-implant question directly. Torabinejad and colleagues found survival of single implants was slightly higher than endodontic microsurgery at 4–6 years, but success-criteria heterogeneity precluded valid comparison and no study made a direct head-to-head [[PMID:25306305]]. Chercoles-Ruiz and colleagues, synthesising 60 studies with ≥1-year follow-up, concluded that endodontic treatment (including retreatment and apical surgery) and implant placement are both valid and complementary, with no important difference emerging until at least 8 years, and rated the recommendation as Level B because of the absence of RCTs [[PMID:28343928]]. Ng and colleagues\' meta-analysis of *nonsurgical* retreatment reported a pooled success rate of 77%, with pre-operative periapical lesion, apical extent of root filling, and quality of coronal restoration as the significant prognostic factors [[PMID:19133093]]. These findings converge on: comparable outcomes, and case-specific factors matter more than the choice of modality.\n\n**Level II — Prospective Studies**\nThe Toronto Study (de Chevigny et al.), the largest prospective series of orthograde retreatment, found 82% healing in retreated teeth overall, with three preoperative predictors of failure: inadequate quality of the previous root filling (paradoxically *better* prognosis, likely because failure was correctable), presence of perforation, and presence of a radiolucency [[PMID:18215667]]. In teeth with a radiolucency, the previous root-filling quality was again the dominant predictor (86% healed if inadequate, 50% if adequate) — meaning that when a technically well-done retreatment has failed, a third orthograde attempt has a materially worse prognosis, which is the exact scenario in this question.\n\n**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**\nTwo studies bear directly. Chatzopoulos and Wolff\'s 7-year comparative cohort of 1,850 procedures found no significant difference in survival between endodontic retreatment and implant re-implantation after multivariable adjustment (aHR 1.25, 95% CI 0.98–1.60, p = 0.08); the dominant predictors of failure were diabetes (aHR 2.50), tobacco use (aHR 1.85), age per decade (aHR 1.18), and maxillary arch (aHR 1.45) [[PMID:41455543]]. Afrashtehfar and colleagues\' cohort of 15,637 teeth demonstrated that teeth *adjacent* to implants had a 10-year survival of 89.2% vs 99.3% for non-adjacent teeth (OR 13.15 for loss), and the leading cause of loss of the neighbours was root fracture (45.2%) [[PMID:39154079]] — a meaningful downstream cost of extracting rather than saving. Doyle and colleagues\' matched comparison of 196 implants vs 196 initial nonsurgical root canal treatments found similar failure rates (6.1% in each), with implants requiring more post-treatment interventions [[PMID:16934623]]. Azim and colleagues\' Buffalo cohort of endodontic microsurgery reported 93% survival and 86–91% success at recall (excluding VRF cases), with major intraoperative procedural error as the only significant predictor — meaning surgical retreatment by an experienced operator is a strong salvage option [[PMID:32975855]]. Olivieri and colleagues\' 2–3-year retrospective retreatment cohort reported 80.6% strict success and 93% loose success, worse in teeth with lesions >5 mm [[PMID:37315780]].\n\n**Level IIIb — Case-Control**\nTuran Gökduman and colleagues\' 5-year cohort of 408 retreated teeth reported 73.5% survival and, among survivors, 79% success, with coronal restoration quality and pre-retreatment obturation quality as significant predictors; root or crown fracture accounted for two-thirds of extractions [[PMID:41062676]]. This is consistent with the Level II Toronto data and reinforces that restorability is decisive.\n\n**Specialty Guidelines & Position Statements**\nThe ACP position on dental implants explicitly considers when an implant is preferred to retaining a tooth (ACP, 2014) and the ACP position on extraction of asymptomatic natural teeth (ACP, 2016) both caution against reflexive extraction of a savable tooth [ACP position statements]. Neither converts survival parity into a preference for either modality; both frame the choice as a case-by-case restorability and risk assessment.\n\n**Level V — Expert Opinion / Reviews**\nSetzer and Kim\'s review reinforces that the natural tooth, when restorable and endodontically manageable, remains the biological default, and that microsurgery has changed the salvage calculus [[PMID:24065635]]. Avila and colleagues propose a decision chart that weighs endodontic, restorative, periodontal and patient factors before extraction, and warn that inadequate extraction indications have historically sacrificed savable teeth [[PMID:19254132]].\n\n---\n\n## NOT FROM THE RETRIEVED EVIDENCE BASE — clinical framework\n\nStandard practice, not from any paper retrieved here: the operational decision usually turns on five checks that the retrieved literature supports as *relevant* but does not package into a rule. (1) *Restorability* — ferrule ≥1.5–2 mm of sound tooth structure circumferentially after caries removal; if absent, the tooth is not a candidate for a third attempt. (2) *Cause of the retreatment failure*, ideally on CBCT — a missed canal (e.g., MB2), a coronal leak, a short or under-condensed obturation, or a small lateral perforation are correctable by surgical microsurgery or selective root retreatment; a vertical root fracture, a strip perforation, or a heavily resected root are not. (3) *Periodontal status* — attachment loss, furcation involvement, and crown-to-root ratio. (4) *Systemic and behavioural risk* — diabetes and smoking degrade both endodontic and implant outcomes, per the retrieved cohort data. (5) *Site factors* — adjacent tooth condition, bone volume, aesthetic zone, patient age and remaining life expectancy of the restoration. Guideline bodies (AAE, ESE, ACP) frame this as a shared decision and specifically advise against defaulting to extraction on the assumption that implants are more durable. °\n\nReturning to what the retrieved evidence supports: when the tooth is restorable and the cause of failure is addressable, apical microsurgery with MTA root-end filling and ultrasonic preparation carries ~86–93% success/survival at medium-term follow-up [[PMID:32975855]] [[PMID:27759881]], which is not meaningfully different from single-implant survival at comparable time points [[PMID:41455543]] [[PMID:28343928]] — and preserves the adjacent teeth whose loss risk rises sharply once an implant is placed next to them [[PMID:39154079]].\n\n---\n\n## REFERENCES\n\n1.
