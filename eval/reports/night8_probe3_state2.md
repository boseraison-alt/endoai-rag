Based on Level I and Level IIIa evidence, the decision should not default to extraction simply because retreatment has failed — a mature molar with a failed non-surgical retreatment still has a legitimate salvage pathway through endodontic microsurgery, which achieves ~91% pooled success in RCTs and ~78% in prospective cohorts at 2–13 years, and 81.5% healing at 10 years with MTA root-end filling [[PMID:32899437]] [[PMID:30827766]]. Extraction and implant becomes the preferred choice when the tooth is unrestorable, has a vertical root fracture, has inadequate ferrule / crown-root ratio, has a combined endo-perio lesion, or when patient-level factors (uncontrolled diabetes, smoking) equally jeopardise both options — because those same systemic factors dominate the failure risk of either salvage attempt [[PMID:41455543]] [[PMID:36683563]].\n\nAn additional consideration the clinician must weigh: extracting this molar and placing an implant creates a documented 13-fold higher risk of loss for the *adjacent* teeth, primarily from root fracture, over ~10 years [[PMID:39154079]] [[PMID:38742582]]. This shifts the calculus toward tooth retention when the adjacent dentition is itself endodontically treated or heavily restored.\n\n---\n\n## EVIDENCE SUMMARY\n\n**Cochrane Reviews**\nThe 2016 Cochrane review by Del Fabbro et al. addressing surgical vs non-surgical retreatment for post-treatment apical periodontitis included only two RCTs directly comparing the approaches (n=126) and found no clear superiority of either at 1-year or 4–10-year follow-up, though evidence quality was very low [[PMID:27759881]]. It did find better healing when MTA was used as the root-end filling material versus smoothed orthograde gutta-percha (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.14–2.24), and weak evidence favouring ultrasonic root-end preparation. The review does not address the tooth-vs-implant question directly, but establishes that surgical retreatment is a legitimate second-line option with a defined evidence base.\n\n**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**\nThe Sinsareekul et al. 2025 systematic review of 8 observational studies comparing endodontically treated teeth (ETT) with implant-supported prostheses reached a genuinely divided conclusion: three studies showed no survival difference in the first 3 years but declining ETT survival thereafter, while three others reported *lower* survival and *more* complications for implants [[PMID:38443242]]. Patient-reported quality of life favoured endodontic treatment. Chercoles-Ruiz et al. (2017) reviewed 60 articles and concluded that single-implant survival exceeded conservative treatment success rates but that no meaningful difference emerged before ~8 years [[PMID:28343928]]. Torabinejad et al. (2015) found implants had higher survival than endodontic microsurgery at 4–6 years but could not compare success rates because criteria differed [[PMID:25306305]]. The Pinto et al. 2020 meta-analysis of endodontic microsurgery outcomes reported a pooled success rate of 91.3% in RCTs and 78.4% in prospective studies over 2–13 years — highly relevant here because microsurgery is the salvage option for a failed retreatment [[PMID:32899437]]. Ng et al.\'s systematic review of secondary root canal treatment reported a pooled success rate of 77% [[PMID:19133093]].\n\n**Level II — Prospective Studies**\nNg et al.\'s prospective cohort of 858 secondary RCT cases (postgraduate operators) reported 4-year cumulative tooth survival of 95.3% following non-surgical retreatment — nearly identical to primary treatment survival [[PMID:21366627]]. Von Arx et al.\'s 5-year prospective follow-up of 191 apical microsurgery cases showed 75.9% healed at 5 years (down from 83.8% at 1 year), with MTA outperforming SuperEBA as root-end filling material [[PMID:22515881]]. The Toronto study (de Chevigny et al.) found 82% healing after orthograde retreatment across four phases, with root filling quality, perforation, and pre-existing radiolucency as significant predictors [[PMID:18215667]].\n\n**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**\nLópez-Valverde et al.\'s 5- to 37-year retrospective cohort of 598 ETT reported cumulative survival of 97%, 81%, 76%, and 68% at 10, 20, 30, and 37 years, respectively — demonstrating that retained ETT can perform extremely well over decades [[PMID:36933044]]. Van Nieuwenhuysen et al.\'s 25-year cohort of 2500 treatments found survival ~90% at 5 years and 50% at 20 years, with combined endo-perio lesions (HR 3.37) and pre-existing complications (HR 1.67) being the dominant predictors of extraction [[PMID:36683563]]. Von Arx et al.\'s 10-year follow-up of 119 apical surgery cases with MTA reported 81.5% healed — supporting microsurgery as a durable salvage option after failed retreatment [[PMID:30827766]]. Chatzopoulos & Wolff\'s 7-year comparative cohort of 1250 endodontic re-treatments vs 600 implant re-implantations found no significant difference in survival after adjusting for baseline factors (aHR 1.25, 95% CI 0.98–1.60); diabetes (aHR 2.50) and tobacco use (aHR 1.85) were the dominant predictors of failure regardless of treatment modality [[PMID:41455543]]. Two large cohorts (Afrashtehfar et al., Chen et al.) reported that teeth adjacent to dental implants have a 13-fold higher risk of loss, primarily from root fracture, over ~10 years — a critical consideration when the neighbouring teeth themselves have prior endodontic or restorative compromise [[PMID:39154079]] [[PMID:38742582]]. Olivieri et al.\'s cohort of nonsurgical retreatments reported 80.6% success under strict criteria at 2–3 years, with molars and teeth with lesions >5 mm having poorer outcomes [[PMID:37315780]]. Restrepo-Restrepo et al. reported only 53.6% success for treatment of teeth with pre-operative apical periodontitis, with mandibular location, lesion size >10 mm, poor coronal restoration, and lack of magnification as key predictors [[PMID:31211862]].\n\n**Level IIIb — Case-Control**\nTuran Gökduman et al.\'s large retrospective study of 408 non-surgical retreatments followed ≥5 years reported survival and success rates both of 73.5%, with coronal restoration quality and initial obturation status as key prognostic factors; root/crown fractures accounted for 66.7% of extractions [[PMID:41062676]]. De Kuijper et al. found 91.7% cumulative survival at 89 months for molars requiring complex endodontic treatment, with deviant canal morphology and inadequate coronal seal reducing healing probability [[PMID:33617944]]. Oh et al. reported that teeth with RCT following crown lengthening had a 6.3-fold higher hazard of loss than non-RCT teeth [[PMID:38874285]].\n\n**Level V — Expert Opinion / Reviews**\nSetzer & Kim\'s review emphasises that survival and success are not interchangeable outcomes, and that modern endodontic microsurgery has substantially improved long-term prognosis for retained teeth [[PMID:24065635]]. Avila et al. proposed a decision-making chart integrating tooth-level and patient-level factors for the retain-versus-extract decision [[PMID:19254132]].\n\n**Guideline context (not tier-ranked)**\nThe ACP position statement on extraction of asymptomatic natural teeth to facilitate prosthodontic treatment reinforces that extraction of a tooth for prosthetic convenience — including implant placement — should be justified, not defaulted to [[PMID:ACP-ASYMPTOMATIC-EXTRACTION-2016]]. This applies here: the patient\'s *request* for extraction does not by itself constitute a clinical indication.\n\n---\n\n**A framework for this decision (standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base):**\nNot from the retrieved evidence base, but reflecting AAE and ESE consensus positions: the retain-vs-extract decision for a molar with failed retreatment turns on (1) restorability — presence of ≥1.5–2 mm ferrule, absence of vertical root fracture, adequate crown-to-root ratio; (2) periodontal status — attachment level, furcation involvement, mobility; (3) endodontic factors — whether the failure has an identifiable, correctable cause (missed canal, coronal leak, iatrogenic error) that microsurgery or selective root retreatment could address; (4) strategic value — position in arch, opposing dentition, role in a planned prosthesis; and (5) patient factors — diabetes control, smoking, bruxism, medications affecting bone healing. °\n\nWhen restorability and endodontic salvageability are both favourable, the retrieved evidence supports attempting endodontic microsurgery before proceeding to extraction, because 5- to 10-year healing rates of 76–91% are reproducibly reported [[PMID:32899437]] [[PMID:22515881]] [[PMID:30827766]]. When restorability is compromised or a correctable cause of failure cannot be identified, extraction and implant placement is the defensible path, provided the patient understands the elevated risk this creates for the adjacent teeth [[PMID:39154079]].\n\n---\n\n## REFERENCES\n\n1.
