('## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION\n\nBased on Level I and Level IIIa evidence, this decision should not be framed as "retreatment vs implant" but as a case-by-case risk assessment, because direct comparative trials do not show clear superiority of either option. Nonsurgical retreatment achieves ~78–87% success and orthograde retreatment after failed apicectomy carries an ~85% success rate [[PMID:38280514]] [[PMID:37096494]], while single implants show higher long-term survival than teeth managed with endodontic microsurgery at 4–6 years [[PMID:25306305]]; however, after adjusting for patient-level risk factors (diabetes, smoking, age, arch), the survival of endodontic re-treatment and implant re-implantation is statistically equivalent [[PMID:41455543]]. For a mature molar with a failed retreatment, the decision should therefore rest on (1) restorability and remaining tooth structure, (2) periodontal attachment level, (3) identifiable and correctable cause of the previous failure (missed canal, coronal leakage, short/overextended fill), and (4) patient-level risk factors — and where these favour the tooth, apical microsurgery is a legitimate third option with 86–94% short-term and ~67–83% long-term success [[PMID:40637350]] [[PMID:35292312]] before proceeding to extraction.\n\n---\n\n## EVIDENCE SUMMARY\n\n**Cochrane Reviews**\nThe 2016 Cochrane review by Del Fabbro et al. compared surgical versus non-surgical retreatment across 20 RCTs and found no clear superiority of either approach for periapical healing at 1 year (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35) or at 4–10 years, though the evidence quality was very low [[PMID:27759881]]. More participants reported pain in the first week after surgery (RR 3.34). No Cochrane review directly compares retreatment or surgery to extraction-plus-implant, which is a genuine gap in the highest-tier evidence.\n\n**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**\nThe most directly relevant systematic review is Bucchi et al. (2023), which restricted analysis to studies comparing surgical and non-surgical retreatment head-to-head across 529 teeth with follow-up 6 months to 8.7 years; apical surgery showed seemingly better periapical healing and less need for further intervention, but tooth survival was higher in the non-surgical group, and heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis [[PMID:35762859]]. Sabeti et al. (2024) pooled contemporary non-surgical retreatment outcomes at 78% (strict) to 87% (loose criteria), with lesion size, apical fill length, and follow-up duration as significant predictors [[PMID:38280514]]. For the implant comparator, Chercoles-Ruiz et al. found that single-implant survival exceeds conservative treatment success rates in the short term, but comparative studies show no important difference until at least 8 years [[PMID:28343928]]; Torabinejad et al. similarly found single implants had higher 4–6-year survival than endodontic microsurgery, but success criteria were qualitatively different and no direct comparisons existed [[PMID:25306305]]. For surgical retreatment, Ibáñez-Aravena et al. (2025) found no significant difference between bioceramics and MTA as root-end fillings (RR 1.00) [[PMID:40637350]], and Sarnadas et al. showed periodontal attachment loss is a significant negative predictor of microsurgery outcome (OR 3.14 for absence of attachment loss) [[PMID:34577845]]. Torabinejad et al. (2009) found surgery had higher 2–4-year success (77.8% vs 70.9%) but non-surgical retreatment had higher 4–6-year success (83.0% vs 71.8%), showing a crossover effect [[PMID:19567310]].\n\n**Level II — Prospective Studies**\nNg et al.\'s landmark prospective study of 750 secondary root canal treatments reported 80% periapical healing at tooth level, with 11 identifiable prognostic factors including preoperative lesion size, apical patency, apical extent of cleaning, and quality of coronal restoration [[PMID:21366626]]; tooth survival at 4 years was 95%, essentially identical to primary treatment [[PMID:21366627]]. von Arx et al. reported 5-year apical microsurgery success of 75.9% (versus 83.8% at 1 year), with interproximal bone level and root-end filling material as significant predictors [[PMID:22515881]]. The Toronto study of orthograde retreatment reported 82% healing pooled across phases, with root filling quality, absence of perforation, and absence of radiolucency as predictors [[PMID:18215667]].\n\n**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**\nThe most directly relevant recent paper is Chatzopoulos & Wolff (2026), a 7-year cohort of 1250 endodontic retreatments versus 600 implant re-implantations: after adjusting for baseline health differences, treatment modality was no longer a significant predictor of failure (aHR 1.25, 95% CI 0.98–1.60, p=0.08), while diabetes (aHR 2.50), tobacco use (aHR 1.85), age, and maxillary arch were the dominant predictors [[PMID:41455543]]. Salehrabi & Rotstein reported 89% tooth retention 5 years after orthograde retreatment in a very large insurance database (n=4744) [[PMID:20416420]]. Olivieri et al. reported 80.6% strict / 93% loose success at 2–3 years for warm gutta-percha retreatments, with lesion size >5 mm being the main negative predictor [[PMID:37315780]]. Olcay et al. reported 85.1% success for multi-visit retreatment at ~34 months [[PMID:30969392]]. For the surgical alternative, Pallarés-Serrano et al. showed microsurgery success drops from 86.9% at 1–4 years to 67.2% at 5–9 years, or 92.5% to 82.6% when vertical root fractures are excluded [[PMID:35292312]]; Azim et al. reported 88% success on periapicals and 86% on CBCT [[PMID:32975855]]. Appel et al. showed orthograde retreatment after failed apicectomy achieves 84.8% success — meaning a second orthograde attempt is a legitimate option even after failed surgery [[PMID:37096494]]. Dawson et al.\'s Swedish register study found that 20% of root-filled teeth were extracted over 10–11 years, with molars and directly restored teeth extracted more often, but only 3.5% underwent formal retreatment and 1.4% root-end surgery [[PMID:38492798]]. Importantly, Afrashtehfar et al. reported that teeth adjacent to implants had a 13-fold higher risk of tooth loss (primarily from root fracture) than non-adjacent teeth [[PMID:39154079]] — a downstream cost of implant placement that must be weighed.\n\n**Level IIIb — Case-Control**\nTuran Gökduman et al. (2025) followed 408 retreated teeth for ~78 months and reported 73.5% survival and 79% success among surviving teeth, with restoration type and initial obturation quality as the main predictors [[PMID:41062676]]. Smith et al. showed that retreatment outcomes are strongly operator-dependent — teeth treated by dentists with postgraduate endodontic training had significantly better healing and 60-month survival (96% vs 90.5%) than those treated in a routine care pathway [[PMID:38512015]]. Baltieri et al. reported 92.5% loose-criteria success for non-surgical retreatment using foraminal enlargement and 2% chlorhexidine at ~30 months [[PMID:39313095]].\n\n**Level V — Expert Opinion / Reviews**\nGulabivala and Ng\'s synthesis reframes outcome prediction: retreatment outcomes depend dominantly on host/infection dynamics, the operator\'s ability to shift the microbial ecology, and the restoration\'s ability to prevent recontamination — the technical procedure is only one input among many [[PMID:36710532]]. Hepworth & Friedman\'s earlier review reported weighted success of 66% for orthograde retreatment and 59% for apical surgery, but noted many older studies are of limited current relevance [[PMID:9170752]].\n\n**Specialty Guidelines & Position Statements**\nThe AAE Treatment Standards White Paper (2018, US) and AAE Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form (2022, US) are both listed here as pointer records — position not quoted [GL:AAE-TREATMENTSTANDARDS-2018 · AAE — Treatment Standards White Paper (with 2019 Executive Summary) (2018; current; US)] [GL:AAE-CASEDIFFICULTY-2022 · AAE — Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form and Guidelines (2022; current; US)]. Standard practice (AAE, ESE, and prosthodontic consensus) treats the following as decisive against saving the tooth and toward extraction/implant: (1) inadequate ferrule / non-restorable coronal breakdown, (2) vertical root fracture, (3) advanced periodontal attachment loss with unfavourable crown-to-root ratio, (4) strategic considerations (opposing occlusion, prosthetic plan), and (5) no identifiable, correctable cause of the previous failure. Factors favouring another endodontic attempt (orthograde re-retreatment or apical microsurgery) include a restorable tooth with adequate ferrule, intact periodontium, and an identifiable cause of prior failure such as a missed canal, short fill, or coronal leakage. Patient factors — smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, and bruxism — worsen prognosis of BOTH options, not just one.\n\nReturning to what the retrieved evidence base does support for the decision in front of this clinician: where the tooth remains restorable and the previous failure has a correctable cause, another endodontic attempt is legitimate — orthograde re-retreatment achieves ~78–87% success and even orthograde retreatment after failed apicectomy achieves ~85% success [[PMID:38280514]] [[PMID:37096494]]; where retreatment has already exhausted the orthograde option, apical microsurgery is the next legitimate step with 86–94% short-term healing [[PMID:32975855]]; and after adjusting for patient risk factors, endodontic re-treatment and implant re-implantation carry statistically equivalent long-term survival, so the patient\'s preference for extraction is a defensible choice — not an obviously superior one [[PMID:41455543]].\n\n---\n\n## REFERENCES\n\n1.
