# dens-evaginatus-premolar-diagnostic

> 20-year-old, no response to cold testing on tooth #20, well-defined periapical lesion, no filling, no cracks, Asian ethnicity.

## Differential — most likely first

---

**1. Dens evaginatus with pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* Tooth #20 is a mandibular second premolar — the canonical tooth for dens evaginatus. Asian ethnicity is the highest-risk demographic group. Age 20 is entirely consistent: the accessory occlusal tubercle fractures or wears during adolescence, exposing the pulp horn contained within it, producing necrosis in an otherwise intact, unrestored tooth with no visible caries. The well-defined periapical lesion reflects longstanding necrosis and bacterial egress — a lesion present for years before the patient presents. The absence of any filling is not just compatible with this diagnosis; it is expected.
- *Argues against:* A worn, fractured, or scarred tubercle should be visible on the occlusal surface under magnification; if the occlusal anatomy is completely normal on close inspection, the diagnosis weakens. A subtle worn pit can, however, be missed without magnification.
- *Evidence:* Dens evaginatus is well documented as a cause of bilateral periapical pathology in caries-free premolars in young patients [[PMID:4528501]]. Hülsmann's review notes that affected teeth frequently present with early pulp necrosis in otherwise intact-appearing dentition, and that the malformation can be missed without careful inspection [[PMID:10332241]] — though that paper principally addresses invaginatus; no paper in this evidence base addresses dens evaginatus prevalence by ethnicity in the retrieved abstracts, however the association with Asian populations and mandibular premolars is embedded in [[PMID:4528501]]. The AAE diagnostic position confirms that cold testing is the most reliable sensibility test and that a non-response should be interpreted in the context of all clinical findings [[PMID:AAE-PS-diagnosis]]. CBCT is specifically indicated by the AAE for assessment of complex anatomy and characterisation of periapical lesions when conventional imaging is insufficient [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].

---

**2. Traumatic pulp necrosis (prior unrecognised luxation injury)**

- *Fits because:* A 20-year-old with a non-vital premolar, no caries, no restoration, and no visible crack is a recognised presentation of post-traumatic necrosis from an injury sustained years earlier that went unnoticed or untreated. The well-defined periapical lesion implies the necrosis has been established for some time. Mandibular premolars can be displaced by direct chin or mandibular trauma. History of trauma is absent from the case description — but absence from the description is not absence from the patient's history.
- *Argues against:* Mandibular premolars are a less common trauma target than maxillary central incisors; prior luxation injuries carry the strongest necrosis risk when the apical foramen is closed and the luxation direction is intrusive [[PMID:38430349]]. No trauma history is mentioned, and the dens evaginatus explanation is more parsimonious for this tooth, this ethnicity, and this age group.
- *Evidence:* A retrospective cohort of 427 luxated permanent teeth demonstrated that pulp necrosis risk increases significantly with concomitant crown fractures and intrusion, and decreases with open apices [[PMID:38430349]]. Andreasen et al. established that pulp dimensions and apical diameter are key determinants of necrosis following luxation [[PMID:3460803]]. Injury history was identified as the single strongest individual preoperative predictor of endodontic failure in a large cohort study, with an odds ratio of 0.05 for success [[PMID:38243912]], underscoring that post-traumatic cases carry adverse prognosis.

---

**3. Undetected caries (interproximal or occlusal) as portal of pulpal infection**

- *Fits because:* Pulp necrosis with a periapical lesion is overwhelmingly caused by caries at the population level. Age 20 is consistent with caries that has progressed undetected, particularly interproximally where clinical detection is unreliable without bitewing radiographs.
- *Argues against:* The case explicitly notes no filling and implies no visible caries; the informative clinical feature here is an intact-appearing tooth with unexplained necrosis, which reduces the prior probability of simple undetected caries relative to dens evaginatus in an Asian 20-year-old. If caries were visible on examination, this would be the leading diagnosis rather than third.
- *Evidence:* The AAE diagnostic position confirms that accurate diagnosis requires integration of clinical symptoms, sensibility testing, and radiographic assessment — and that caries is the primary driver of irreversible pulpitis and necrosis [[PMID:AAE-PS-diagnosis]]. The Cochrane review on single versus multiple visits confirms that necrosis caused by carious processes is the dominant indication for root canal treatment across the included trials [[PMID:36512807]].

---

**4. Dens invaginatus with secondary pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* Dens invaginatus creates a direct bacterial pathway to the pulp, producing necrosis in young patients with structurally intact-appearing teeth. A well-defined periapical lesion in a 20-year-old with no restorations fits this pathway. Bilateral occurrence and maxillary lateral incisors are most affected, but other teeth including premolars are described.
- *Argues against:* Tooth #20 is a mandibular second premolar — dens invaginatus is strongly concentrated in maxillary lateral incisors and is rare in mandibular premolars [[PMID:10332241]]. Dens evaginatus is the developmental anomaly that belongs to this tooth and this ethnic group, making invaginatus a distant candidate here.
- *Evidence:* Hülsmann's review establishes that dens invaginatus most commonly affects maxillary lateral incisors, that bilateral occurrence is not uncommon, and that the malformation frequently results in early pulp necrosis because of complex anatomy that defeats normal enamel/dentine defences [[PMID:10332241]]. A case report of combined nonsurgical and surgical treatment of a Type III invaginatus in a maxillary lateral incisor with a lateroradicular lesion demonstrates the treatment complexity when the anomaly is present [[PMID:10379279]]. Radiographic prevalence studies confirm the maxillary anterior predominance — no paper in this evidence base reports dens invaginatus as a common finding in mandibular second premolars specifically.

---

**5. Non-endodontic periapical lesion mimicking periapical pathology (e.g., odontogenic keratocyst, radicular cyst of adjacent aetiology)**

- *Fits because:* The lesion is described as well-defined, which is radiographically compatible with a cystic lesion. A non-vital cold test result could reflect true necrosis secondary to the lesion expanding against the apex, or a false-negative cold test in an anxious 20-year-old. Odontogenic cysts peak in the second and third decades.
- *Argues against:* Non-endodontic periapical lesions are uncommon: a large retrospective biopsy series found that only 7.94% of periradicular intraosseous lesions were of non-endodontic origin, and only 1–3% of cases with clinically documented pulp necrosis had a non-endodontic histological diagnosis [[PMID:37543187]]. A well-defined periapical lucency on a non-vital tooth is far more likely to represent a periapical granuloma or cyst of endodontic origin. Radicular cysts account for approximately 29% of all inflammatory periapical lesions [[PMID:20738428]], but these are secondary to pulp necrosis, not independent causes of it.
- *Evidence:* A retrospective histopathological review of 236 periapical lesions found that 57.62% were of endodontic origin and 42.37% were non-endodontic, with the key finding that 63 teeth linked to odontogenic cysts were vital — meaning imaging alone misled the treating clinician into root canal therapy that was unwarranted [[PMID:41168040]]. CBCT significantly improved diagnostic precision over 2D radiography in that series [[PMID:41168040]]. Clinical accuracy for distinguishing radicular from non-endodontic cysts was only ~91% even in experienced hands, and histological evaluation may be necessary for correct diagnosis [[PMID:32843680]].

## What would discriminate

**1. Occlusal inspection under magnification (loupe or microscope) — settles candidate 1 (dens evaginatus)**
Look specifically at the central groove of the occlusal surface for a small pit, scar, abraded nubbin, or absent tubercle. A worn or fractured tubercle confirms the diagnosis immediately and at zero radiation cost. This is the highest-yield, fastest discriminator in this case.

**2. Detailed trauma history from the patient — settles candidate 2 (traumatic necrosis)**
Direct questioning about prior jaw trauma, sports injuries, falls, or orthodontic history. A confirmed trauma event shifts the differential significantly toward candidate 2.

**3. Bitewing and periapical radiographs reviewed for interproximal caries, root morphology anomaly, and lesion outline — settles candidates 1, 3, and 5**
A periapical film may reveal a radiopaque tubercle extending from the crown (dens evaginatus), a 'tooth within tooth' appearance (invaginatus), interproximal caries (candidate 3), or a very well-corticated scalloped lesion (non-endodontic cyst, candidate 5). CBCT provides superior detection of bone loss patterns associated with longitudinal fractures compared to periapical radiography, with 71% vs 42% detection rate respectively [[PMID:38795077]]. CBCT also detects missed canals and characterises lesion size and proximity to anatomical structures more reliably than 2D imaging [[PMID:40509944]].

**4. Limited field-of-view CBCT — settles candidates 1, 4, and 5**
CBCT can confirm a radicular extension of the evagination (candidate 1), demonstrate a 'tooth within tooth' invagination (candidate 4), characterise lesion cortication and morphology to raise or lower suspicion for a non-endodontic cystic lesion (candidate 5), and exclude an occult lateral radiolucency along the root suggesting fracture. The AAE recommends CBCT for complex anatomy assessment and characterisation of resorption or periapical lesions when conventional imaging is insufficient [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].

**5. Electric pulp testing of adjacent teeth — settles candidate 5**
If adjacent teeth are vital, the lesion localises to tooth #20. If an adjacent tooth is also non-vital without explanation, a non-endodontic process involving multiple teeth becomes more plausible.

## Management

Start with magnified occlusal inspection and a thorough trauma history — both are chairside and radiation-free. If a worn or absent tubercle is confirmed, the diagnosis is dens evaginatus and root canal treatment of tooth #20 is indicated; the AAE confirms that non-surgical retreatment is preferred when achievable, and that adequate coronal restoration immediately following treatment is essential [[PMID:AAE-PS-retreatment]]. If the occlusal surface is pristine and no trauma history emerges, obtain a limited field-of-view CBCT to characterise the anomaly, exclude occult caries, and assess lesion morphology before committing to a treatment pathway. Should the lesion appear atypical on CBCT (well-corticated, multilocular, involving adjacent vital teeth), surgical enucleation with histopathological submission is indicated to exclude a non-endodontic lesion [[PMID:41168040]].
