# dens-evaginatus-premolar-diagnostic

> 20-year-old, no response to cold testing on tooth #20, well-defined periapical lesion, no filling, no cracks, Asian ethnicity.

intent: diagnostic  |  papers: 68  |  cost: $0.212

---
**Differential — most likely first**

---

**1. Dens evaginatus with pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* Tooth #20 is the mandibular left second premolar — the single most commonly affected tooth in dens evaginatus. Asian ethnicity is the highest-risk demographic. The tooth has no caries, no restoration, and no reported crack, yet is non-vital with a well-defined periapical lesion: this is the classical presentation of DE, where the evaginated tubercle wears or fractures flush with the surrounding enamel and is not recognised as a portal of entry. A 20-year-old is exactly the age at which cumulative tubercle attrition has had time to cause pulp necrosis and periapical breakdown, but the patient is young enough that no secondary caries has supervened.
- *Argues against:* No tubercle or wear facet on the occlusal surface is mentioned — but this absence from the history is not the same as absence from the tooth. A fully-worn tubercle is flush and easily overlooked without directed magnified examination; the "intact" appearance is precisely the clinical trap.
- *Evidence:* Dens evaginatus is a developmental anomaly producing a supernumerary tubercle on the occlusal surface of premolars; its prevalence is markedly higher in East and Southeast Asian populations and it preferentially involves mandibular premolars, leading to pulp necrosis and periapical pathology when the tubercle is abraded [[PMID:4528501]]. The AAE diagnostic position statement emphasises that accurate pulpal diagnosis requires integration of sensibility testing and radiographic findings, and that an absent cold response combined with periapical pathology in an intact tooth warrants investigation for developmental aetiology [[PMID:AAE-PS-diagnosis]]. CBCT is specifically indicated when conventional imaging is insufficient to explain the presenting pathology and to characterise complex or unexpected root canal anatomy [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].

---

**2. Remote, unrecalled or unrecognised traumatic injury causing pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* No caries and no restoration in a 20-year-old with necrosis and a mature periapical lesion forces consideration of trauma as the second most common cause of pulp death in young patients. A concussion or subluxation sustained years earlier may have been dismissed or genuinely forgotten. The well-defined lesion indicates the process has been evolving for some time, consistent with remote injury. Mandibular premolars are not the classic trauma zone but are not immune.
- *Argues against:* Anterior teeth sustain the overwhelming majority of traumatic luxation injuries. Tooth #20 is posterior, making this a considerably less likely mechanism than in an anterior tooth. There is no mention of crown discolouration, which often accompanies post-traumatic pulp necrosis, nor of root resorption.
- *Evidence:* In a retrospective cohort of 427 luxated permanent teeth, pulp necrosis occurred in 28.1% of cases overall, with the type of luxation, the diameter of the apical foramen, and the presence of concomitant crown fractures as the main determinants of pulp prognosis [[PMID:38430349]]. A smaller apical foramen (as in a fully mature tooth) is associated with reduced capacity for revascularisation after injury [[PMID:3460803]]. History of injury was the single strongest pre-operative predictor of unsuccessful root canal treatment outcome (OR 0.05) in a large private-practice cohort, underscoring how a missed trauma history distorts clinical reasoning [[PMID:38243912]].

---

**3. Dens invaginatus with pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* Although dens invaginatus classically affects maxillary lateral incisors, it can occur in premolars and mandibular teeth; it produces early pulp necrosis because the invagination creates a direct or near-direct communication between the oral environment and the pulp, often before caries is detectable. The "no caries, no restoration, no crack" picture with necrosis in a young patient is characteristic. Asian ethnicity has been associated with higher DI prevalence in some series.
- *Argues against:* Tooth #20 (mandibular second premolar) is not the canonical site; maxillary lateral incisors account for the substantial majority of cases [[PMID:10332241]] [[PMID:41184566]]. A premolar DI would be unusual but is not impossible and should not be dismissed in this demographic without radiographic exclusion.
- *Evidence:* Dens invaginatus results from infolding of the dental papilla during tooth development, most often affects maxillary lateral incisors, but shows a spectrum of morphologic variation and frequently results in early pulp necrosis due to the complex anatomy [[PMID:10332241]]. Radiographic screening of 10,000 patients found DI prevalence of 7.07%, with maxillary lateral incisors predominantly affected (65.8%) and no mandibular involvement detected, illustrating that premolar involvement is atypical but the anomaly is common enough to warrant exclusion [[PMID:41184566]].

---

**4. Occult cracked tooth with pulp necrosis**

- *Fits because:* A deep, incomplete crack can allow bacterial ingress and cause pulp death without forming a cavity detectable by naked-eye inspection. Mandibular premolars bear significant occlusal load, particularly in a patient whose first molar may have been extracted, shifting occlusal forces. The case states "no cracks" but this reflects inspection, not transillumination or staining.
- *Argues against:* The case explicitly records no cracks. A crack severe enough to cause full necrosis and a periapical lesion in a 20-year-old is uncommon without some clinical sign (pain on biting, wedging pain, localised deep pocket). Dens evaginatus is substantially more likely than an occult crack in this tooth, this patient, and this demographic.
- *Evidence:* The AAE cracked tooth position statement identifies transillumination, dye staining, and bite testing as the required discriminatory examinations; it notes that prognosis is guarded when the crack involves the pulp floor and that early diagnosis with cuspal coverage improves retention [[PMID:AAE-PS-cracked-tooth]]. CBCT imaging detects bone loss patterns associated with longitudinal tooth fractures significantly better than periapical radiographs (71% vs 42%), providing additional diagnostic information when a crack is suspected but not visible clinically [[PMID:38795077]].

---

**5. Non-endodontic periapical lesion mimicking inflammatory periapical pathosis (e.g., odontogenic keratocyst, cemento-osseous dysplasia, non-odontogenic cyst)**

- *Fits because:* A "well-defined" periapical radiolucency is the radiographic hallmark of cystic and tumour-like lesions, not merely of chronic apical granulomas. In a 20-year-old with an otherwise intact tooth and a round or scalloped lesion, a developmental or neoplastic lesion that secondarily abuts the apex must be excluded before irreversible treatment is rendered.
- *Argues against:* Cold-testing negative on tooth #20 confirms absence of pulp vitality, which strongly supports a true endodontic aetiology for the periapical change. A non-endodontic lesion mimicking a periapical lesion would more typically be associated with a vital tooth. In a large histopathology review, only ~8% of periradicular biopsies were of non-endodontic origin, and only 1–3% in cases with clinically reported pulp necrosis [[PMID:37543187]]. Cemento-osseous dysplasia in its osteolytic stage can mimic a periapical cyst but occurs predominantly in middle-aged Black women, making it a poor demographic fit here, though CBCT texture analysis can discriminate between the two [[PMID:38605361]].
- *Evidence:* A retrospective study of 236 histopathological periapical lesion reports found 42.4% were non-endodontic in origin (including odontogenic cysts, tumours, and fibro-osseous lesions), and critically, 26.8% of teeth linked to odontogenic cysts were vital, indicating that some apparently "endodontic" periapical lesions do not warrant root canal therapy [[PMID:41168040]]. Clinical differentiation between endodontic and non-endodontic periapical cystic lesions achieves approximately 91.8% accuracy, but histopathological evaluation remains necessary in atypical cases [[PMID:32843680]].

---

**What would discriminate**

1. **Magnified occlusal examination with transillumination** — settles *Candidate 1 (dens evaginatus)* immediately; look for a central pit, worn tubercle remnant, stained fissure, or flat scar on the occlusal surface of tooth #20. Takes under one minute and requires no additional equipment beyond a good light and a 3.5× loupe.

2. **Periapical radiograph (parallel technique, close inspection)** — settles *Candidates 1 and 3*: DE shows a radiopaque projection from the occlusal surface or a prominent pulp horn pointing occlusally; DI shows the pathognomonic "tooth-within-a-tooth" invagination; also screens for root resorption (*Candidate 2*), canal obliteration (post-trauma *Candidate 2*), or a corticated cyst wall extending beyond the apex (*Candidate 5*).

3. **Directed trauma history** — settles *Candidate 2*; specifically ask about falls, sports injuries, or blows to the face in childhood and adolescence. Also assess for crown discolouration, which accompanies remote post-traumatic pulp necrosis.

4. **Transillumination and bite test (Tooth Sleuth/Fractfinder)** — settles *Candidate 4 (cracked tooth)*; a positive bite test or light-blocking crack line discriminates crack from DE remnant.

5. **Limited FOV CBCT** — settles *all remaining candidates* if the above steps are inconclusive: confirms DE tubercle or DI morphology in cross-section, identifies crack lines or radicular groove anatomy, characterises lesion boundaries (corticated dome = cyst, irregular = granuloma), and detects cortical expansion or multilocularity suggesting a non-endodontic process (*Candidate 5*) [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]] [[PMID:40509944]].

---

**Management — briefly**

Treat this as dens evaginatus until the clinical examination proves otherwise: look first, radiograph second, CBCT third. If DE is confirmed (tubercle remnant, characteristic radiograph, or CBCT morphology), the tooth requires root canal treatment given the established necrosis and periapical lesion, followed by full-coverage cuspal protection; success rates for primary endodontic treatment of teeth without pre-operative apical periodontitis exceed 92–98%, though this tooth already has a lesion, placing the prognosis in the 68–85% healed category at four years [[PMID:ESE-PS-VPT-2019]] [[PMID:2084204]]. If trauma history is elicited and DE is excluded, management is the same endodontic pathway. If the lesion appears atypical on CBCT (cortical expansion, non-apex-centred, vital adjacent teeth), refer for surgical biopsy before committing to root canal treatment, as approximately 8% of periradicular lesions are non-endodontic in origin [[PMID:37543187]].
