Differential — most likely first

1. Dens evaginatus with pulp horn extension

- *Fits because:* Tooth #20 is the mandibular left second premolar — the single most frequently reported tooth for dens evaginatus. The patient is 20 years old and of Asian origin, precisely the demographic and age group in which this anomaly classically presents with pulp necrosis: the evaginated tubercle fractures or abrades during occlusal function in the early teens, silently exposing a pulp horn that tracks through the cusp, leading to necrosis without caries, restorations, trauma, discoloration, or probing defect. The contralateral tooth (mandibular right second premolar, #29) is already root-canal-treated — bilateral premolar involvement without caries or trauma is a hallmark pattern of dens evaginatus. Normal perio probings and absent discoloration are fully consistent.
- *Argues against:* No fractured or worn occlusal tubercle was specifically mentioned — though this can be subtle, flush with the occlusal surface, or already entirely worn away and indistinguishable without magnification or CBCT cross-section.
- *Evidence:* Dens evaginatus is documented as a leading etiology of non-surgical root canal treatment in immature permanent teeth, with dens evaginatus listed as the predominant cause of pulp necrosis in this subset in a large Thai cross-sectional study (n = 1500), ahead of caries and trauma [[PMID:39179988]]. The same study found that bilateral premolar involvement is a characteristic pattern. A classic case report established dens evaginatus as the cause of bilateral periapical pathology in caries-free premolars [[PMID:4528501]]. The AAE diagnostic framework emphasises that pulp necrosis without caries or restoration in a young patient mandates identification of an alternate entry point, and the clinical and radiographic presentation must be integrated systematically [[PMID:AAE-PS-diagnosis]].

2. Occult or buccal-pit caries not visible on clinical or radiographic examination

- *Fits because:* Dental caries is by far the most common overall etiology for non-surgical root canal treatment in permanent teeth [[PMID:39179988]]. Buccal-pit caries in mandibular premolars and tight interproximal lesions can be missed on standard periapical films and visual examination, particularly if the clinician was not specifically looking for them with transillumination or bitewing radiography at an altered angle. Young, otherwise intact-looking teeth can harbour deep dentinal lesions.
- *Argues against:* The clinician reports no visible caries and no restorations. Normal perio probings exclude a draining sinus through the sulcus. The bilateral premolar pattern and Asian origin make an occult developmental anomaly considerably more parsimonious. If bitewing or CBCT were already taken and showed no caries, this candidate falls further down the list.
- *Evidence:* Caries accounts for 53.7% of root canal treatment indications in a large cross-sectional study, making it the dominant etiology overall [[PMID:39179988]]. The AAE position statement notes that periapical radiolucency without an identifiable pulpal entry point requires systematic clinical and radiographic examination to exclude all carious or restorative pathways [[PMID:AAE-PS-diagnosis]]. CBCT is indicated when conventional imaging is insufficient to characterise the cause of periapical pathology [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].

3. Dens invaginatus (type I or II)

- *Fits because:* Dens invaginatus causes silent pulp necrosis in young patients via a developmental infolding that permits bacterial ingress without caries or trauma. Although the maxillary lateral incisor is the classic site, mandibular premolars are occasionally affected; the anomaly is more prevalent in populations of Asian origin. The presentation — necrosis in a young male with no caries, no restoration, no trauma, no discoloration, normal probings — is identical to the dens evaginatus pattern and dens invaginatus must be on the list until imaging excludes it.
- *Argues against:* Dens invaginatus overwhelmingly affects maxillary laterals (65.8% of cases in one large series) [[PMID:41184566]] and mandibular premolar involvement is uncommon. Dens evaginatus is a more parsimonious explanation for a mandibular premolar in an Asian patient. Periapical radiography often shows the characteristic invagination shadow; if this was not noted, the diagnosis is less likely but cannot be excluded without CBCT.
- *Evidence:* Dens invaginatus has a pooled global prevalence of approximately 7% and maxillary lateral incisors account for the large majority of cases; mandibular involvement is rare but documented [[PMID:41184566]]. Dens invaginatus is a recognised cause of early pulp necrosis due to a direct bacterial pathway through the invagination [[PMID:10332241]]. Asymptomatic nonvitality is specifically noted as a characteristic feature, and periapical lesions were observed in 22 of 170 affected teeth in a large retrospective series [[PMID:38748387]]. CBCT is the investigation of choice to characterise the invagination anatomy and plan treatment [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].

4. Unrecognised or forgotten prior trauma

- *Fits because:* Luxation injuries in young males are common and can be minimised or forgotten, particularly if they did not cause immediate pain. Pulp necrosis after luxation can be delayed by months to years and present without discoloration if it occurs slowly. The contralateral premolar's root canal treatment history could also reflect a prior bilateral traumatic event. Pulp necrosis risk after luxation is strongly influenced by severity of displacement and apex maturity [[PMID:3460803]].
- *Argues against:* The patient explicitly denies trauma. There is no discoloration — chronic post-traumatic necrosis in a young tooth characteristically causes grey-brown crown discoloration [[PMID:2699588]]. No crown fracture, infraction, or mobility is mentioned. History of injury has been identified as a strongly negative prognostic factor in root canal treatment outcomes (OR 0.05), suggesting it is a distinct and identifiable category [[PMID:38243912]], which argues that if it were present it would usually be recalled. Dens evaginatus is a far more parsimonious bilateral premolar explanation.
- *Evidence:* Pulp necrosis following luxation injuries is strongly associated with severity of displacement and closed apices; open apices are protective [[PMID:38430349]]. Pulpal healing patterns after luxation were characterised in a large longitudinal series demonstrating that necrosis can occur without crown discoloration in a minority of cases, though discoloration is the dominant presenting sign [[PMID:2699588]]. The relationship between pulp dimensions and post-luxation necrosis is established, with narrower apices carrying greater risk [[PMID:3460803]].

5. Radicular developmental groove on the premolar root

- *Fits because:* Developmental grooves are not exclusive to maxillary laterals. A mandibular premolar with a narrow radicular groove can permit bacterial ingress to the pulp without caries, restoration, or obvious probing defect in early stages, especially if the groove does not yet communicate with the periodontium at a detectable depth. The 'nothing to see' presentation is consistent.
- *Argues against:* Radicular grooves in mandibular premolars are uncommon. The large multicentric CBCT study found radicular groove prevalence in mandibular first premolars at approximately 20% globally [[PMID:40410308]], but clinical significance sufficient to cause pulp necrosis without any probing abnormality is rare. Normal probings throughout argue against a groove-mediated perioendo lesion, which typically shows at least one narrow localised probing depth.
- *Evidence:* Radicular grooves in mandibular first premolars have a pooled global prevalence of approximately 20% with considerable regional variation and bilateral symmetry, and shared developmental pathways with C-shaped canals and taurodontism are noted [[PMID:40410308]]. No paper in this evidence base directly addresses radicular groove-mediated pulp necrosis in mandibular second premolars specifically.

What would discriminate

1. High-magnification occlusal examination under loupes or microscope for a fractured/worn evaginated tubercle — settles candidate 1 (dens evaginatus): a remnant nipple, a stained pit at the tubercle base, or a flush wear facet on the buccal cusp is pathognomonic. Highest yield per unit of chair time.
2. Limited field-of-view CBCT of the premolar region — settles candidates 1, 3, and 5 simultaneously: cross-sectional slices will show a pulp horn projecting into an evaginated cusp (candidate 1), the classic teardrop invagination within the root (candidate 3), or a linear groove on the root surface (candidate 5). The AAE recommends CBCT when clinical and conventional imaging are insufficient to characterise the etiology of periapical pathology [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].
3. Bitewing radiography at an altered horizontal angulation — settles candidate 2 (occult interproximal or buccal-pit caries) if not already obtained; adds minimal chair time and radiation.
4. Detailed trauma history including collateral history from family members, and inspection of old photographs or dental records — settles candidate 4; look for evidence of splinting, missing time from school, or facial injury in the patient's early to mid-teens.
5. Careful periodontal probing of all six surfaces under magnification with methylene blue staining — settles candidate 5 (radicular groove); a narrow isolated probing depth or visible groove staining confirms it.

Management (brief)

Establish the etiology before planning the endodontic access, because a dens evaginatus tooth has complex pulp anatomy that requires CBCT-guided access preparation to instrument fully. Once etiology is confirmed, standard non-surgical root canal treatment is appropriate for the necrotic tooth with periapical periodontitis; success rates exceed 86% for necrotic teeth with periapical lesions and improve significantly with technically adequate obturation [[PMID:2084204]]. If CBCT confirms dens evaginatus or dens invaginatus, the contralateral tooth's treatment history reinforces the bilateral developmental aetiology and no further systemic workup is needed. If all structural causes are excluded after CBCT, a medical history review for systemic inflammatory conditions should be initiated before accepting an idiopathic diagnosis.
