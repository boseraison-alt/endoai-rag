> **User:** oh i do see a possible dens evaginatu sin tooth #20. Is there anything a dentist can do to present pulp necrosis from setting in?

---

**Assessment:** With a visible dens evaginatus (DE) tubercle on tooth #20 (mandibular left second premolar) in a young patient, the clinical priority shifts to prevention — the tubercle is at high risk of fracture from occlusal loading, and once the pulp horn within it is exposed, necrosis follows rapidly given the thin dentinal walls and direct bacterial pathway to the pulp.

---

**Recommendation:** Intervene prophylactically based on tubercle integrity and pulp horn extension. The specific approach depends on what you see clinically and on CBCT:

1. **If the tubercle is intact and the pulp horn extends into it (confirm with CBCT cross-section):** Electively reduce the tubercle in small increments over multiple appointments to stimulate reparative/tertiary dentine deposition before the pulp horn recedes naturally. Place a desensitising or calcium silicate–based agent (e.g., Biodentine or MTA) over the reduced surface after each increment to protect the pulp. This controlled, staged reduction is standard practice for DE management and is not derived from a specific RCT in this evidence base — label it accordingly: **standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base.**

2. **If the tubercle is intact but the pulp horn does NOT extend far into it (shallow horn on CBCT):** Apply fissure sealant or composite resin over the tubercle to reinforce it against fracture and seal the potential bacterial ingress point. This is a lower-risk, entirely non-invasive first step. Splinting a fragile tubercle with flowable composite or sealant to prevent accidental fracture is widely practised in paediatric and general dentistry for DE.

3. **If the tubercle is already fractured but pulp is still vital:** Immediate direct pulp cap or partial pulpotomy with a calcium silicate cement (MTA or Biodentine) gives the best chance of preserving vitality. Tri-calcium silicate materials achieve a mean success rate of 86.8% (95% CI: 83.78%–89.82%) in vital pulp therapy with carious or traumatic pulp exposure in mature teeth [[PMID:41339865]], compared to only 34.3% for calcium hydroxide [[PMID:41339865]]. Immediate restoration after the pulp cap is critical — each day of delay reduces success by a measurable margin [[PMID:41339865]].

4. **Occlusal adjustment:** Reduce the opposing occlusal contact on the tubercle to remove the primary fracture force. This is low-risk, irreversible but minor, and can be combined with any of the above. Do this at every stage.

---

**Evidence:**

- Partial pulpotomy with tri-calcium silicate cements (MTA, Biodentine) achieves an 86.8% success rate in mature teeth with pulp exposure, significantly outperforming calcium hydroxide (34.3%), with immediate restoration timing being an independent predictor of success [[PMID:41339865]].
- The overall success rate of pulpotomy for permanent teeth with carious or traumatic pulp exposure across 25 RCTs is 86.7% (95% CI: 82.0%–90.7%), with MTA and Biodentine outperforming calcium hydroxide as capping materials (MTA OR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.28–4.51) [[PMID:38968236]].
- In teeth with reversible pulpitis or asymptomatic pulp exposure, partial pulpotomy achieved a 91.5% success rate at 12 months versus 81.3% for direct pulp capping (difference not statistically significant), supporting partial pulpotomy as at least equivalent and potentially superior when intraoperative bleeding assessment guides the choice [[PMID:40617285]].
- No significant difference in clinical or radiographic outcome was found between partial and full pulpotomy at 1 year in mature molars with irreversible pulpitis (88% vs 91.6%, p > .05), meaning that even if DE has caused more extensive pulp involvement, a conservative coronal approach remains valid before committing to root canal treatment [[PMID:36403208]].
- CBCT is the investigation of choice before any prophylactic intervention on a DE tooth: it characterises how far the pulp horn extends into the tubercle, guides the depth of any elective reduction, and confirms whether the contralateral tooth has a similar anatomy requiring the same prophylaxis [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].
- Dens evaginatus is the leading developmental etiology of pulp necrosis in premolars in populations of Asian origin, and bilateral involvement is a documented pattern — the already root-canal-treated contralateral tooth (#29) in this patient illustrates the natural history if prophylaxis is not applied [[PMID:39179988]].
- Once pulp necrosis sets in, success rates for root canal treatment of necrotic teeth with periapical lesions drop to 86% compared to >96% for vital cases, underscoring the value of prevention [[PMID:2084204]].

---

**Key Considerations:**

- **CBCT before any reduction:** Do not electively grind the tubercle without a CBCT cross-section of tooth #20. If the pulp horn extends to within 1–2 mm of the tubercle tip, any reduction without concurrent calcium silicate coverage risks direct pulp exposure. The AAE recommends limited FOV CBCT when conventional imaging is insufficient to characterise complex anatomy [[PMID:AAE-PS-cbct]].
- **Staged reduction protocol:** Each increment should be small (0.5 mm or less), followed by placement of a calcium silicate liner and a sealed composite restoration, then re-evaluation at 6–8 weeks for sensitivity and vitality before proceeding — **this interval and increment size reflect standard DE management practice; they are not derived from a specific paper in the retrieved evidence base.**
- **Check the contralateral (#29) tooth's records:** If its DE was managed only after necrosis, this confirms the family history of late diagnosis and reinforces aggressive prophylaxis now.
- **Assess the other premolars:** Bilateral symmetry is high for DE [[PMID:40410308]]. Examine and consider CBCT of all four premolars, particularly #21 (mandibular left first premolar) and maxillary premolars.
- **Pulp sensibility monitoring:** After any intervention, schedule 3- and 6-month electric pulp test and cold test recall appointments. Loss of response is the earliest detectable sign that necrosis is beginning, giving a window for vital pulp therapy before full necrosis sets in. Combining both tests significantly improves detection sensitivity over either alone [[PMID:39810326]].
- **Red flag for immediate escalation:** If at any recall visit the tooth is non-responsive to both EPT and cold test, transition immediately to root canal treatment — do not delay, as the existing periapical lesion on the contralateral side demonstrates what untreated necrosis produces in this patient's biology.
