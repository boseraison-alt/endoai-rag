# naocl-concentration

> Sodium hypochlorite concentration low versus high and outcome of primary root canal treatment

intent: None  |  papers: 70  |  cost: $1.1436

## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

Based on Level I and higher-tier evidence, **low-concentration NaOCl (0.5–3%) and high-concentration NaOCl (≥5%) produce equivalent primary root canal treatment outcomes** — tooth survival, radiographic healing of apical periodontitis, and bacterial reduction are indistinguishable at short- and long-term follow-up [[PMID:39133080]] [[PMID:35579074]] [[PMID:22972129]]. Lower concentrations are associated with less post-endodontic pain and reduced analgesic requirement without compromising success [[PMID:38880472]]. A pragmatic protocol is to use 1–3% NaOCl for routine cases, compensating for reduced tissue-dissolving kinetics with adequate volume, contact time, warming, and mechanical/ultrasonic activation, and reserving higher concentrations (5–5.25%) only where a demonstrable clinical rationale exists (e.g., heavy necrotic tissue burden) while employing rigorous apical-safety measures.

## EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Cochrane Reviews**
The Fedorowicz Cochrane review (2012, 11 RCTs, 851 participants) directly addressed irrigant choice and concentration for non-surgical root canal treatment in mature permanent teeth and concluded there is insufficient reliable evidence to demonstrate superiority of any one irrigant or concentration; no included trial showed differences in swelling, pain, or bacterial culture attributable to NaOCl concentration, and no trial reported radiographic periapical outcomes [[PMID:22972129]]. This remains the highest-tier synthesis available and its central finding — no demonstrated clinical superiority of higher over lower NaOCl concentrations — has not been overturned by subsequent evidence.

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**
The most directly relevant long-term clinical evidence is Dahlstrand Rudin's 6-year follow-up of a quasi-randomised trial (n=271) comparing 0.5% versus 3% NaOCl, which found no significant difference in tooth survival (85.6% vs 81.1%), retreatment incidence, symptom resolution, or absence of radiographic apical periodontitis (74.0% vs 64.1%, p=.20) [[PMID:39133080]]. Rossi-Fedele and Rödig's systematic review similarly found no significant difference in AP reduction between 1% and 5% NaOCl and no difference in 7-day postoperative pain across concentrations [[PMID:35579074]]. Two 2024/2026 meta-analyses of RCTs on post-endodontic pain reach a nuanced position: Prasad et al. (5 RCTs, 674 patients) reported significantly less pain and less analgesic use with ≤3% versus ≥5% NaOCl (OR ~2.3–2.5) with low-to-moderate certainty [[PMID:38880472]], whereas Teja et al. (7 RCTs) found no statistically significant difference at 24/48/72 h but a consistent trend favouring <5% [[PMID:41733459]]. Immich et al. found no concentration-dependent difference in pain reduction or radiographic lesion resolution [[PMID:39868615]]. Ruksakiet's meta-analysis showed NaOCl and CHX have equivalent antimicrobial efficacy, supporting the view that bacterial reduction is not the limiting variable that discriminates concentrations [[PMID:32413440]]. Nasr's 2026 RCT (n=60) found domestic-grade (unstandardised, higher-concentration) NaOCl produced significantly more early pain than medical-grade formulations, consistent with the concentration-pain relationship [[PMID:41975390]]. The Inchingolo and Fiorillo reviews caution that severe complications from apical extrusion are disproportionately linked to higher-concentration NaOCl mishandling [[PMID:41681109]] [[PMID:39418527]].

**Classic / Foundational**
The Byström and Sundqvist bacteriological studies established that 0.5% NaOCl is more effective than saline at reducing intracanal bacteria and that even low concentrations achieve substantial disinfection when combined with adequate mechanical preparation and volume [[PMID:6572884]] [[PMID:3922900]]. Sjögren's landmark work demonstrated that the presence of residual bacteria at obturation — not the specific NaOCl concentration used — is the dominant predictor of long-term failure (94% success with negative culture vs 68% with positive culture) [[PMID:9477818]], reframing the concentration debate around whether the protocol achieves microbial reduction, not the reagent strength per se.

**Level II — Prospective Studies**
No prospective study in this evidence base directly compares NaOCl concentrations on primary treatment outcome; the available Level II data address sealer performance and irrigant activation rather than the concentration question [[PMID:40491343]] [[PMID:42163172]].

**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**
Konadu et al. compared 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.6%, and 5.2% NaOCl for microbial reduction in single-rooted single-canal teeth and reported that all four concentrations produced substantial microbial reduction, with 2.6% and 5.2% eliminating all cultivable organisms and 0.5%/1.0% leaving residual organisms in only 2 canals [[PMID:38976717]]. This supports a concentration-related trend for microbiological completeness of disinfection but not for clinical outcome.

**Level IIIb — Case-Control**
Beşkoç et al. reviewed 60 irrigant-related soft-tissue injuries and identified NaOCl as responsible for 53.3%, with significant associations with necrosis and scarring — reinforcing that the safety margin narrows as concentration rises, particularly among less experienced operators [[PMID:42126068]].

**Level V — Expert Opinion**
Boutsioukis and Arias-Moliz's authoritative review concludes NaOCl remains the primary irrigant of choice and that no adjunct method has been shown to improve long-term outcome beyond adequate instrumentation plus syringe irrigation, with concentration being one of several variables (volume, contact time, temperature, agitation) that can be optimised [[PMID:35338652]]. Zehnder's classic review notes that increasing concentration escalates caustic potential disproportionately to gains in clinical efficacy and advocates optimising delivery rather than concentration [[PMID:16631834]]. Haapasalo et al. state higher concentrations dissolve tissue more effectively in vitro but that the balance between efficacy and safety, especially apically, argues for caution [[PMID:24651335]].

**Summary of concordance and disagreement**
The Cochrane review, the largest and longest clinical follow-up (Dahlstrand Rudin, 6 years), and the most recent systematic reviews converge: primary root canal treatment outcomes are not measurably improved by using ≥5% NaOCl over 0.5–3%. Where the literature diverges is on postoperative pain — Prasad's meta-analysis found a significant advantage for lower concentrations, while Teja's meta-analysis found only a non-significant trend in the same direction — but both point in the same direction and neither shows harm from lower concentrations. The trade-off is well-characterised: higher concentrations offer greater tissue-dissolution kinetics in vitro but no proven clinical outcome benefit, with a higher documented risk of extrusion injury.
