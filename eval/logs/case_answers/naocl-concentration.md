## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

For primary root canal treatment of mature permanent teeth, use **low-concentration sodium hypochlorite (0.5%–3%)** rather than high concentrations (≥5%). Level I evidence — including a 6-year quasi-randomised clinical trial and multiple systematic reviews — demonstrates equivalent long-term clinical, radiographic, and antimicrobial outcomes between low and high concentrations, while low concentrations produce significantly less postoperative pain and lower risk of catastrophic hypochlorite accidents [[PMID:39133080]] [[PMID:38880472]] [[PMID:38819357]]. Reserve higher concentrations only when enhanced tissue-dissolution is clinically required, and in those cases combine with strict safety measures (rubber dam, side-vented needle, working length control) [[PMID:41681109]].

## EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Cochrane Reviews**
The 2012 Cochrane review by Fedorowicz et al. (11 RCTs, 851 participants) concluded there is insufficient reliable evidence to demonstrate superiority of any single irrigant or concentration for clinically relevant outcomes such as pain, swelling, or radiographic healing [[PMID:22972129]]. Bacterial culture reductions did not translate into differences in patient-centred outcomes, and the review specifically called for trials reporting long-term success. This remains the highest-tier synthesis but is now more than a decade old and is complemented by more recent RCTs and SRs below.

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**
The most direct long-term clinical comparison is the 6-year follow-up of a quasi-randomised trial by Dahlstrand Rudin et al. (n=298), which found **no statistically significant difference** between 0.5% and 3% NaOCl for tooth survival (85.6% vs 81.1%), retreatment rates, or radiographic absence of apical periodontitis (74.0% vs 64.1%, p=0.20) [[PMID:39133080]]. This is the strongest evidence that low-concentration NaOCl does not compromise the primary outcome of root canal treatment. Rossi-Fedele and Rödig's SR similarly found no significant difference in apical periodontitis reduction between 1% and 5% NaOCl and no difference in 7-day postoperative pain [[PMID:35579074]], and Immich et al.'s complementary SR reached the same conclusion across additional time points [[PMID:39868615]].

For postoperative pain, two 2024–2026 systematic reviews reach broadly concordant but nuanced conclusions. Prasad et al. (5 RCTs, 674 patients) found low concentrations (≤3%) were associated with significantly less pain at 24 h (OR 2.32) and 48 h (OR 2.49) and reduced analgesic use, though with low-to-moderate certainty [[PMID:38880472]]. Teja et al. (7 RCTs) found no statistically significant difference in pooled analysis but noted that lower concentrations trended toward less pain in most included studies [[PMID:41733459]]. The Vitali et al. RCT (n=154) provides direct evidence: 8.25% NaOCl increased postoperative pain scores 3.48-fold versus 2.5% in necrotic mandibular molars [[PMID:38819357]]. Meta-analyses comparing NaOCl to chlorhexidine consistently find no antimicrobial superiority of either agent [[PMID:32413440]], and safety-focused SRs emphasise that the incidence and severity of hypochlorite accidents rises with concentration [[PMID:41681109]] [[PMID:39418527]].

**Classic / Foundational**
Byström and Sundqvist's foundational bacteriological studies established that even 0.5% NaOCl provides meaningful antimicrobial action superior to saline [[PMID:6572884]] [[PMID:3922900]], and Sjögren et al. demonstrated that residual infection at obturation reduces success from 94% to 68%, highlighting that antimicrobial adequacy — not concentration per se — drives outcome [[PMID:9477818]]. These studies underpin the modern position that adequate volume, contact time, and mechanical activation matter more than raw concentration.

**Level II — Prospective Studies**
Arias et al. (n=500) identified predictors of post-endodontic pain using 5% NaOCl but did not compare concentrations directly [[PMID:23402273]]. No Level II study addresses concentration as its primary variable for outcome.

**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**
Konadu et al. compared 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.6%, and 5.2% NaOCl in a Ghanaian cohort and found the higher concentrations (2.6% and 5.2%) eliminated all cultivable microorganisms whereas 0.5% and 1.0% left persistent streptococci in a small number of cases — suggesting a modest microbiological advantage of higher concentrations, though clinical outcome was not assessed [[PMID:38976717]]. This provides the main counterpoint to a "low-is-equivalent" position at the microbiological (not clinical) level.

**Level IIIb — Case-Control**
Beşkoç et al. retrospectively analysed 60 soft-tissue injury cases and found NaOCl responsible for 53.3% of injuries, with strong associations with necrosis (43.7%) and scarring (75%) — reinforcing that concentration-related safety risk is a clinically meaningful factor [[PMID:42126068]].

**Level V — Expert Opinion**
Boutsioukis and Arias-Moliz conclude that NaOCl remains the primary irrigant of choice but that no adjunctive method or specific concentration has demonstrated improved long-term treatment outcomes beyond what standard instrumentation and syringe irrigation achieve [[PMID:35338652]]. Haapasalo et al. state high-concentration NaOCl has better tissue-dissolving effect than 1–2% solutions but acknowledge safety trade-offs [[PMID:24651335]].
