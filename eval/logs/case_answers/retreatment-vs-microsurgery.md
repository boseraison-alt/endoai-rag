## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

**The literature is currently divided on this topic.** Based on Level I evidence, nonsurgical retreatment (NSR) and apical microsurgery achieve broadly comparable long-term healing rates for persistent apical periodontitis, so the choice should be driven by case-specific factors — restorability, quality of existing root filling, post/coronal restoration presence, lesion size, root anatomy, and patient preference — rather than a universal preference for one modality [[PMID:10726528]] [[PMID:18091714]] [[PMID:29075023]]. Where the existing root filling and coronal restoration are inadequate or a correctable intracanal cause is identifiable, orthograde retreatment is the logical first step; where a well-condensed root filling with an intact coronal restoration coexists with persistent periapical disease, contemporary endodontic microsurgery with MTA or a bioceramic root-end filling delivers success rates of ~85–94% and is a defensible primary choice [[PMID:31078325]] [[PMID:32483680]] [[PMID:34687565]].

## EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Cochrane Reviews**

The Cochrane-derived synthesis by Ferrailo and Veitz-Keenan concluded there is no clear evidence of superiority for either surgical or nonsurgical retreatment in periapical lesion healing at 1-year (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35; two RCTs, 126 participants) or at 4–10 years, though the evidence base was rated very low quality [[PMID:29075023]]. Surgically treated patients reported significantly more pain in the first postoperative week. Within surgical protocols, ultrasonic root-end preparation and MTA root-end fillings showed modest advantages, but the review explicitly stated that available evidence does not provide reliable guidelines for choosing between the two approaches.

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**

The seminal Kvist and Reit RCT (n=95, 4-year follow-up) found that surgical retreatment healed faster (significantly higher success at 12 months) but converged with NSR at 48 months, with four early "healed" surgical cases reclassifying as late failures — highlighting that short follow-up systematically favours surgery [[PMID:10726528]]. The Del Fabbro and Taschieri systematic review reached the same conclusion: 1-year functional success was 90.7% (surgery) vs 80.6% (NSR), but by 4 years outcomes were similar, and the choice should rest on case-specific factors including operator skill, technical feasibility, and patient preference [[PMID:18091714]]. Baseri et al.'s meta-analysis of 44 studies found that periapical lesion size did not significantly affect success of RCT, NSR, or apical surgery overall (RRs 1.04–1.11, all CIs crossing 1), though long-term RCT subgroup analysis did favour smaller lesions [[PMID:36890256]]. On the surgical side, Safi et al.'s RCT (n=120, 15-month follow-up) reported ~93% PA and ~85% CBCT success for endodontic microsurgery with no difference between MTA and EndoSequence RRM [[PMID:31078325]]. de Lange et al.'s RCT (n=290 with follow-up) demonstrated that ultrasonic root-end preparation improved outcomes over bur preparation (80.5% vs 70.9%, significant in molars) [[PMID:17900941]]. Gulsever et al.'s small RCT suggested L-PRF adjunct with either apicoectomy or curettage achieved >90% lesion area healing at 9 months [[PMID:40057596]]. Nadzharyan et al. found MTA and glass ionomer as retrograde fillings performed comparably at 12 months [[PMID:41379411]].

**Level II — Prospective Studies**

Taha et al. reported 93% success at 1 year for microsurgery with a premixed tricalcium silicate putty (n=50) [[PMID:32483680]]. Wang et al.'s prospective microsurgery cohort (n=74 analysed) achieved a 90.5% combined complete/incomplete healing rate at 12–30 months, with use as a prosthetic abutment identified as a significant negative predictor [[PMID:28292605]]. Boronat-López et al. reported a 94.4% success rate at 12 months for anterior maxillary apical surgery with submarginal incision, with minimal periodontal or cortical changes [[PMID:40818137]]. Gagliani et al.'s 5-year longitudinal study is important because it stratified by prior surgical history: primary apical surgery achieved 86% complete healing, but surgical *retreatment of previously operated teeth* dropped to 59% (p=0.001) — a factor rarely considered in the NSR-vs-surgery debate [[PMID:15876296]]. Peñarrocha et al. (n=333 teeth) found smaller preoperative lesions and lesser apical resection improved prognosis [[PMID:17656278]]. Bornstein et al. showed that both 2D and CBCT imaging correlate poorly with histologic diagnosis (only ~5% of "cysts" on imaging were true cysts histologically), a caveat when case-selecting for surgery based on suspected cystic lesions [[PMID:25863407]].

**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort Studies**

Weissman et al.'s retrospective cohort (n=384, 48-month follow-up) reported a 90.6% surgical retreatment success rate at 4 years, with 12-month status closely predicting long-term outcome and only minor late regression [[PMID:34687565]]. Olcay et al. reported 85.1% success at ~34 months for multiple-visit nonsurgical retreatment (n=101 analysed), with mandibular first molars most likely to fail [[PMID:30969392]]. Signor et al. (n=321) found periapical lesion area, tooth type, and apical resorption significantly associated with retreatment failure, and identified large lesions plus poor primary root filling quality as key predictors of healing failure [[PMID:33886941]]. Zamparini et al.'s 8-year cohort of failing endodontically treated premolars compared retreatment with extraction/implant: 8-year survival was 85.1% (Endo) vs 98.2% (Implant), with root fracture — driven by insufficient coronal structure — the main reason for endo failure, not persistent periapical disease [[PMID:40074937]]. von Arx et al.'s review of 330 referrals showed that even among cases referred *for* apical surgery, 25.8% were ultimately deemed non-retainable and extracted, reinforcing that periodontal status and restorability are decisive [[PMID:24461402]].

**Level IIIb — Case-Control Studies**

Martí-Bowen et al. (n=100 canals) reported 84.2% global success at 12 months for ultrasonic apical surgery with amalgam retrofill, with no significant effect of lesion size, apical resection depth, or retrofill cavity size [[PMID:15800469]]. Small case series of intentional replantation as a third option for persistent lesions where both NSR and surgery are contraindicated showed acceptable short-term outcomes [[PMID:32855480]].

**Level V — Expert Opinion / Reviews**

Blum's contemporary review of post-treatment endodontic disease frames the decision as three options — observation, retreatment (surgical or nonsurgical), or extraction — with case selection driven by aetiology, restorability, and expected outcome [[PMID:40151135]]. Petean et al.'s decision-making framework recommends CBCT reassessment for persistent PAI 5 lesions, with CBCTPAI score guiding the choice between nonsurgical retreatment (score 3) and endodontic surgery (scores 4–5) [[PMID:39836228]].

> *Not from the retrieved evidence base: standard case-selection heuristics endorsed by AAE and ESE position statements favour NSR when a correctable intracanal cause is identifiable (missed canal, inadequate obturation, coronal leakage, iatrogenic error that can be bypassed) and the coronal restoration can be removed without compromising restorability. Apical microsurgery is favoured when the existing root filling is technically adequate, when a well-fitting post or high-value coronal restoration would be jeopardised by disassembly, when perforations or ledges cannot be negotiated orthogradely, or when biopsy is indicated. These are consensus principles rather than trial-derived — verify against the current ESE S3 guideline or AAE case difficulty assessment when planning.*

Returning to what this evidence base supports: for the typical persistent apical periodontitis case, both modalities are legitimate first-line options with comparable long-term outcomes, and the load-bearing decision variables are restorability, quality of the existing root filling, and lesion/anatomic factors — not a universal ranking of one modality over the other [[PMID:10726528]] [[PMID:29075023]].
