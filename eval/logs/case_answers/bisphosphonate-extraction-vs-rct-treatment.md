# bisphosphonate-extraction-vs-rct-treatment

> 68-year-old with osteoporosis on alendronate, needs a decision between extraction and root canal treatment on a lower molar with a large periapical lesion

intent: treatment  |  papers: 39  |  cost: $0.1273

---
**Assessment:** A 68-year-old on oral bisphosphonate therapy (alendronate) with a large periapical lesion on a mandibular molar represents a moderate-risk scenario where the consequences of extraction — specifically medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) — must be weighed carefully against the feasibility and prognosis of root canal treatment.

**Recommendation:** Retain the tooth with root canal treatment rather than extraction. The evidence consistently supports root canal treatment as the safer, tooth-preserving option in bisphosphonate-exposed patients with periapical disease. If RCT fails to heal the lesion long-term, apical surgery is available as a secondary option before extraction is considered.

**Evidence:**

Zamparini et al. (2021) prospectively followed 109 root canal-treated teeth in bisphosphonate patients over 60 months and found a survival rate of 85% and success rate of 76% — not significantly different from the bisphosphonate-naïve control group (88% survival, 73% success; P > 0.05) [[PMID:32901962]]. Crucially, no MRONJ occurred in any root canal-treated patient in that cohort, making RCT a demonstrably safe approach in this population.

Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated that even with an optimised extraction protocol — including minimally invasive technique, concentrated growth factor placement, and antibiotic prophylaxis — 248 teeth were extracted from anti-resorptive agent patients with zero MRONJ at 90 days, though this represents only a very short follow-up window and the risk window for MRONJ extends well beyond 3 months [[PMID:41366443]]. This underscores that extraction carries inherent risk even with best practice, and avoidance is preferable.

Regarding the large periapical lesion specifically: Sjogren et al. (1990) established that teeth with pulp necrosis and periapical radiolucency achieve only 86% healing after primary RCT, falling further to 62% for previously root-filled teeth — reinforcing that lesion size and pre-operative periapical status are the key prognostic determinants, not patient age or bisphosphonate use per se [[PMID:2084204]]. Juric et al. (2024) confirmed in a 1,259-tooth cohort that increasing PAI score and lesion diameter >5mm significantly reduce success odds (OR 0.24 for lesions ≥6mm vs. no lesion), so realistic prognosis counselling is essential [[PMID:38243912]].

For the large periapical lesion, Dogan et al. (2024) showed that all advanced irrigation activation methods (PUI, SWEEPS, MDA) produced significant periapical volume reduction at 12 months in large lesions, without statistically significant differences between techniques — even manual dynamic activation produced meaningful healing [[PMID:38878107]]. If surgical intervention becomes necessary, Dhamija et al. (2024) demonstrated that 3D CBCT-assessed healing of large through-and-through periapical lesions continued to improve from 66.7% at 1 year to 83.3% at 5 years after microsurgery, supporting a watch-and-wait approach before committing to surgery [[PMID:38758526]]. The Cochrane review by Del Fabbro et al. (2016) found no clear superiority of surgical versus non-surgical retreatment at 1-, 4-, or 10-year follow-up for apical periodontitis, supporting a non-surgical first approach [[PMID:27759881]].

The AAE position statement on retreatment supports non-surgical RCT as the first-line option before apical surgery when tooth structure permits [[PMID:AAE-PS-retreatment]].

Endodontic treatment of apical periodontitis also carries systemic benefit: Jakovljevic et al. (2025) demonstrated significant reductions in serum hs-CRP at 6 months and IL-1β at 12 months following successful endodontic treatment, which is clinically relevant given that systemic inflammation may interact with bisphosphonate-mediated bone metabolism [[PMID:39540337]].

Regarding material selection during RCT: Juric et al. (2024) found that bioceramic sealers outperformed resin-based sealers in treatment outcomes (OR 0.58 for resin sealer as a failure predictor), making bioceramic-based obturation the preferred choice in this case [[PMID:38243912]].

**Key Considerations:**

- **MRONJ risk stratification:** Alendronate is an oral bisphosphonate with lower MRONJ risk than intravenous agents (zoledronate). Duration of therapy matters — patients on oral bisphosphonates for <4 years without risk factors (steroids, diabetes) carry lower risk than those on longer courses. Coordinate with the prescribing physician regarding a drug holiday, though evidence for this reducing MRONJ risk is limited.

- **Prognosis counselling:** Given the large periapical lesion, communicate a realistic healing probability. The ESE outcome data indicates healed rates of 68–85% at 4 years for teeth with pre-existing apical periodontitis; larger lesions trend toward the lower end [[PMID:ESE-PS-VPT-2019]]. Van Nieuwenhuysen et al. (2023) showed success probability declines to approximately 60% at 20 years in a large practice cohort, with lesion size among the strongest predictors of failure [[PMID:36683563]].

- **Multiple visits preferred:** The same cohort study found single-visit treatment was a significant failure predictor in complex cases (OR 0.40 vs. multiple visits) [[PMID:38243912]]. Schedule a minimum of two visits with intracanal calcium hydroxide placement between appointments.

- **Coronal restoration quality is critical:** A defective post-treatment restoration was an independent failure predictor (OR 0.35) [[PMID:38243912]]. For a mandibular molar, full-coverage restoration with a crown is essential after RCT completion.

- **If RCT healing is incomplete at 12–18 months:** Apical surgery with MTA retrograde fill and bone regeneration adjuncts (bone graft ± membrane) is a reasonable next step before extraction. Flynn et al. (2024) showed significantly better apical surgery outcomes when regenerative techniques are used versus surgery alone (OR 2.18) [[PMID:38491954]].

- **Extraction as a last resort:** If extraction ultimately becomes unavoidable, implement a bisphosphonate-safe protocol — atraumatic extraction, socket preservation, primary closure where possible, and extended post-operative monitoring beyond the standard 90-day window.
