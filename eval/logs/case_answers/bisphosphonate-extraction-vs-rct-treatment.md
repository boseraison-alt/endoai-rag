# bisphosphonate-extraction-vs-rct-treatment

> 68-year-old with osteoporosis on alendronate, needs a decision between extraction and root canal treatment on a lower molar with a large periapical lesion

intent: treatment  |  papers: 34  |  cost: $0.1192

---
**Assessment:** This is a high-stakes decision in a bisphosphonate-exposed patient where both extraction and retained infected periapical pathology carry independent MRONJ risk — the goal is to eliminate the endodontic infection while avoiding mucosal breach where possible.

**Recommendation:** Favour nonsurgical root canal treatment (NSRCT) over extraction. Alendronate (oral bisphosphonate for osteoporosis) carries substantially lower MRONJ risk than IV bisphosphonates, but untreated apical periodontitis in a bisphosphonate-exposed patient is itself a recognised MRONJ trigger. Completing NSRCT eliminates the infectious focus, avoids the surgical trauma of extraction, and preserves the alveolar bone envelope. Obtain a CBCT to characterise lesion volume, root anatomy, and cortical plate integrity before committing to a treatment pathway [[PMID:40539414]] [[PMID:41168040]]. If NSRCT is technically feasible (adequate coronal tooth structure, restorable tooth, no vertical root fracture), proceed with NSRCT in a single or staged visit as dictated by the clinical presentation [[PMID:36512807]].

**Evidence:**

Nonsurgical retreatment/primary treatment of teeth with large periapical lesions yields pooled periapical healing rates of approximately 78–87% using contemporary techniques [[PMID:38280514]]. Preoperative lesion size negatively affects outcome, so the large lesion here is a genuine prognostic factor, but successful NSRCT remains the most conservative path [[PMID:2084204]] [[PMID:38280514]]. At a 5-year follow-up, root canal-treated teeth in bisphosphonate patients showed survival rates of 85% and success rates of 76%, statistically comparable to non-bisphosphonate controls (88% survival, 73% success; p > 0.05), confirming that NSRCT is a safe approach in this population [[PMID:32901962]]. Untreated apical periodontitis in the context of antiresorptive therapy significantly increases histological and clinical MRONJ risk in preclinical models, reinforcing the urgency of eliminating the endodontic infection rather than leaving it [[PMID:41390055]]. Hypertensive patients — and by extension patients with multiple comorbidities common in this age group — have significantly increased odds of losing root-filled teeth (OR = 1.78), underlining the importance of careful follow-up [[PMID:38051279]].

Effective endodontic treatment significantly reduces systemic hs-CRP and IL-1β at 6 and 12 months respectively [[PMID:39540337]] [[PMID:42068431]], supporting the systemic benefit of infection elimination beyond local periapical healing.

For large periapical lesions specifically, all irrigation agitation modalities (manual dynamic activation, passive ultrasonic, laser/SWEEPS) produced significant lesion volume reduction at 12 months with no statistically significant difference between techniques in single-rooted teeth, so standard irrigant protocol (NaOCl with final EDTA activation) is appropriate without mandating advanced adjuncts [[PMID:38878107]].

Regarding obturation, sealer-based obturation with calcium silicate sealers and warm vertical compaction show comparable 12–22 month success rates (76.6% vs 80.5% on PAI; 71.3% vs 65.5% on CBCT-PAI) [[PMID:40653155]]. At 24 months, both cold lateral condensation and carrier-based techniques showed modest advantage over single-cone, with no differences beyond 3 years; operator expertise outweighs technique choice [[PMID:41872366]].

CBCT-assessed outcomes reveal lower success under strict criteria (36%) versus loose criteria (88%), highlighting that periapical radiographs will overestimate healing; plan CBCT follow-up for this case given lesion size [[PMID:40122230]].

If NSRCT fails or if the lesion does not regress, apical surgery with MTA retrograde fill is the evidence-supported next step — MTA root-end fill shows significantly better healing than orthograde GP smoothing (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.14–2.24) [[PMID:27759881]], and bone grafting with barrier membrane improves apical surgery healing outcomes overall (OR = 2.18, 95% CI 1.32–4.31) [[PMID:38491954]]. However, apical surgery in a bisphosphonate patient carries additional MRONJ risk and should be deferred unless NSRCT has been given adequate time to heal (minimum 12–24 months radiographic review).

**Key Considerations:**

- **MRONJ risk stratification:** Oral alendronate for osteoporosis carries substantially lower absolute MRONJ risk than IV zoledronic acid for malignancy. Duration of use and any concurrent corticosteroid therapy increase risk. If alendronate has been taken >4 years or steroids are co-prescribed, liaise with the prescribing physician regarding a medication drug holiday (standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base; recommended by the AAOMS position paper on MRONJ, outside this evidence block).
- **Do not extract without serious reason:** Extraction with osteotomy is a strong independent predictor of ORN/MRONJ risk [[PMID:38591808]]; periapical periodontitis is a leading indication for extraction in patients on ARAs, and even with optimal protocol, mucosal healing does not guarantee bone-level safety [[PMID:41366443]].
- **Histopathological caution:** In this age group, large periapical lesions occasionally represent non-endodontic pathology (odontogenic cysts, fibro-osseous lesions, neoplasm). A retrospective series found 42% of lesions submitted at apical surgery were non-endodontic in origin [[PMID:41168040]]. If CBCT morphology is atypical (scalloped, non-periapical epicentre, tooth tests vital), biopsy is mandatory before committing to NSRCT.
- **Restorability assessment first:** Confirm adequate tooth structure for coronal restoration. Tooth type significantly affects survival in bisphosphonate patients — lower molars are mechanically demanding. Tissue-level restorations (subcrestal crown margins reducing occlusal stress) showed comparable outcomes to normal occlusal contacts in the 60-month bisphosphonate cohort [[PMID:32901962]].
- **Follow-up schedule:** Given large lesion size and bisphosphonate exposure, plan clinical and radiographic review at 6, 12, and 24 months minimum. CBCT at 12–24 months is advisable to accurately quantify healing, as periapical radiographs will systematically overestimate resolution [[PMID:40122230]] [[PMID:ESE-PS-VPT-2019]].
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis:** Evidence does not support routine antibiotic prophylaxis for NSRCT in this context (no benefit shown in endodontic surgery RCT, RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.09–2.64) [[PMID:27759881]]; liaise with the physician if the patient is immunocompromised or on high-dose antiresorptive therapy.
