## CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

Assumed: a definitive all-ceramic (or metal-ceramic) crown is being retained after endodontic access, and you are deciding on the coronal seal within the access cavity.

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) alone is **not** an ideal permanent access restoration through a ceramic crown — it is acceptable as an intra-canal orifice barrier under a bonded composite, but the occlusal/external seal in the access opening should be a bonded resin composite, not GIC. Level I in vitro meta-analytic evidence shows GIC, RMGIC, composite resin and MTA all significantly reduce coronal microleakage as orifice barriers with no significant difference between them [[PMID:36661351]], but Level I long-term restorative data show GIC has significantly shorter longevity than resin composite (~80% survival at 6 years vs 85–90% at 10 years) with retention loss and loss of anatomical contour as dominant failure modes — poor properties for the load-bearing external seal [[PMID:35221127]]. The pragmatic protocol supported by the evidence base is a layered restoration: an orifice-level barrier (GIC, RMGIC, or a calcium-silicate/MTA plug) [[PMID:36661351]] sealed externally with a bonded composite; the in vitro systematic review on endodontic access through all-ceramic crowns identified repair protocol, ceramic type, and luting agent as impacting fracture resistance but could not conclusively define a best-practice protocol [[PMID:27542693]].

## EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**

The most directly relevant Level I evidence is a systematic review and meta-analysis on orifice barriers, which pooled 18 in vitro studies (10 in meta-analysis) and found that GIC (RR 0.37), RMGIC (RR 0.32), composite resin (RR 0.54), MTA (RR 0.25) and Cavit (RR 0.23) all significantly reduced coronal microleakage compared with no barrier, with **no statistically significant difference between GIC, RMGIC, composite resin and MTA** [[PMID:36661351]]. This supports GIC as a legitimate *orifice-level* barrier, but the review specifically addressed the barrier over the root filling — not the definitive external restorative seal within the crown's access cavity. A systematic review of in vitro studies on endodontic access through all-ceramic crowns identified crown fabrication method, luting agent, ceramic type and repair protocol as factors affecting post-access fracture resistance, but heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis and no best-practice sealing material could be identified [[PMID:27542693]]. A 10-year meta-analysis of direct posterior restorations found GIC restorations achieved only ~80% survival at 6 years versus 85–90% for resin composite at 10 years, with GIC failures dominated by loss of anatomical contour and retention loss — directly relevant to why GIC is a poor choice for the load-bearing external seal [[PMID:35221127]].

**Level IIIa — Retrospective Cohort**

None of the retrospective cohorts in this evidence base specifically address access restoration through ceramic crowns. One retrospective cohort of 248 endodontically treated teeth found overall RCT success was not significantly influenced by "quality of restoration" category in a comparison of bioceramic vs resin sealers [[PMID:40369057]] — an indirect signal that a well-executed coronal seal performs acceptably, but this does not distinguish materials for the access-through-crown scenario.

**Level V — Expert Opinion**

No expert review in the retrieved set addresses this specific clinical scenario directly.

**Overall evidence quality:** Weak for the specific question. No RCT compares GIC vs composite as the definitive access restoration through an existing ceramic crown. The best available evidence is in vitro and addresses the orifice barrier layer, not the external seal [[PMID:36661351]]. Recommendations therefore lean on general restorative material longevity data [[PMID:35221127]] and consensus guidance from outside the retrieved evidence base.
