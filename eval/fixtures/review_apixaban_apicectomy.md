## Body

### CLINICAL RECOMMENDATION

**COCHRANE EVIDENCE**

The retrieved endodontic evidence base does not directly address perioperative management of apixaban (Eliquis) in patients undergoing apical surgery — no included Cochrane review, RCT, or cohort study evaluates DOAC interruption, bridging, or bleeding outcomes in endodontic microsurgery. What this evidence base does establish is that apical surgery is a soft-tissue and osteotomy procedure whose surgical protocol (incision design, magnification, root-end preparation and filling) is well-standardised and generally low-risk for major bleeding [PMID 27759881], and that adjunctive haemostatic/regenerative agents such as PRF reduce early postoperative bleeding-related morbidity [PMID 42652796].

From the wider literature (which this search did not return, and which should be consulted directly): current SDCEP, BSH, and ACC/AHA guidance is that apicectomy is classified as a dental procedure with low-to-moderate bleeding risk, and for most patients on apixaban monotherapy the drug should not be routinely interrupted. Standard practice is to either (a) proceed without interruption using local haemostatic measures (atraumatic technique, sutures, tranexamic acid 4.8% mouthwash, oxidised cellulose, collagen plug ± PRF), scheduling the procedure early in the day and ≥4 hours after the morning dose; or (b) for higher-bleeding-risk cases (large osteotomy, multiple teeth, difficult access, concurrent antiplatelet therapy, CrCl <50 mL/min, or age >75), consider omitting the morning dose on the day of surgery in consultation with the prescribing physician. Bridging with LMWH is not indicated for apixaban. INR testing is not applicable.

Information that would change the recommendation: indication for apixaban (AF vs VTE vs mechanical valve — although mechanical valves are a contraindication to DOACs), concurrent antiplatelet therapy, renal function (CrCl), age, size and location of the planned osteotomy, and whether non-surgical retreatment is a feasible alternative — the Cochrane review found no clear superiority of surgical over non-surgical retreatment for periapical healing at 1 year, so avoiding surgery altogether in a bleeding-risk patient is a legitimate option [PMID 27759881]. not applicable.

### Evidence summary

**Cochrane Reviews**

The Del Fabbro et al. 2016 Cochrane review of 20 RCTs on endodontic retreatment is the highest-tier source retrieved but does not address anticoagulation [PMID 27759881]. Two included trials compared surgical root-end resection with non-surgical retreatment and found no clear superiority for periapical healing at 1 year (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.97–1.35) or at 4–10 years, though surgically-treated patients reported significantly more pain in the first postoperative week (RR 3.34, 95% CI 2.05–5.43). The review evaluated incision design, magnification, ultrasonic root-end preparation, root-end filling materials (with MTA superior to smoothed gutta-percha, RR 1.60), antibiotic prophylaxis, and grafting — none of the included trials stratified patients by anticoagulant status or reported bleeding complications as an outcome. Clinically, this means the choice between surgical and non-surgical retreatment can legitimately be made on patient-specific factors including bleeding risk, without a strong efficacy penalty for choosing the non-surgical route.

**Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews**

The ESE 2023 Quality Guidelines endorse magnification, CBCT for pre-surgical assessment, and standardised outcome reporting for endodontic surgery but do not provide specific guidance on DOAC management [[PMID:ESE-QG-2023]]. The Valdivieso Del Pueblo et al. 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of PRF in endodontic microsurgery found that PRF-based adjuncts significantly reduce early postoperative pain (SMD −0.62, 95% CI −1.03 to −0.21, p = 0.003) and may accelerate early bone healing, with moderate certainty for postoperative recovery outcomes [PMID 42652796]. PRF is relevant here because autologous platelet concentrates support local haemostasis in the osteotomy site and have a plausible role as an adjunct in anticoagulated patients, although no included trial specifically enrolled DOAC-treated patients. Bucchi et al. 2023 confirmed that neither surgical nor non-surgical retreatment demonstrates clear superiority (5 studies, 529 teeth), reinforcing that non-surgical retreatment remains a defensible alternative when surgical bleeding risk is a concern [PMID 35762859]. Flynn et al. 2024 showed that bone grafting ± membranes improves apical surgery healing outcomes (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.32–4.31) but again did not stratify by medical comorbidity [PMID 38491954].

**Level II–V**

No prospective, retrospective, case-control or case-series evidence in this retrieval addresses apixaban or DOAC management around apicectomy. The retrieved cohort data (López-Valverde et al. 2023; Jurič et al. 2024) report long-term endodontic success predictors but do not include anticoagulation as a variable [PMID 36933044] [PMID 38243912].

### Evidence gap

This search returned no endodontic-specific evidence on DOACs and apical surgery. Perioperative apixaban management for apicectomy should be guided by cardiology/haematology/oral-surgery guidelines (SDCEP Management of Dental Patients Taking Anticoagulants or Antiplatelet Drugs; ACC 2024 perioperative DOAC guidance; ISTH consensus) rather than by the endodontic outcomes literature, which has not studied this population.
