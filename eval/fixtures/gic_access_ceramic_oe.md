**Eleven sources, and not one is a randomised trial.** That is the whole point
of A33: nobody will randomise a patient's ceramic crown, so the best available
evidence for this question IS bench and survey evidence. Curo bands in vitro
second-from-bottom and, before A31, had no tier for surveys or descriptive work
at all — so the literature that answers the question was either at the bottom
of the ladder or outside it.

Rating shown: **D+ / Minimal Evidence**

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) is not recommended as the definitive surface
restoration for an endodontic access opening through a ceramic crown; a bonded
resin composite is the material of choice for access through any porcelain- or
ceramic-containing crown. GIC's role in this setting is best confined to a deep
base, liner, or intraorifice barrier beneath a composite surface layer, rather
than as the occlusal-facing permanent fill. [1-3]

## Why composite, not GIC, at the surface

- **Survey and consensus of practice.** In a survey of general practitioners,
  prosthodontists, and endodontists, resin composite was the reported material
  of choice for restoring access openings through all crown types involving
  porcelain, whereas amalgam (with or without bonding) was preferred for
  all-metal crowns. This reflects both esthetics and the ability to bond
  composite to an etched/silanated ceramic margin. [1]
- **Mechanical properties.** GIC has reduced tensile strength and wear
  resistance relative to composite and amalgam, and it is generally regarded as
  unsuitable as a stand-alone material on occlusal (chewing) load-bearing
  surfaces — it is more often used provisionally. For an access cavity that sits
  directly in occlusion, this is the central limitation. [4]
- **Sealing/microleakage.** A meta-analysis of intraorifice barriers found GIC
  produced significantly greater microleakage than resin-based composite (mean
  difference ~3.96 mm dye penetration favoring composite), which is directly
  relevant to maintaining the coronal seal that protects the root canal
  treatment. Loss of coronal seal is a recognized cause of endodontic
  failure. [5]
- **Bonding to the crown.** Composite can be micromechanically and chemically
  bonded to the ceramic (hydrofluoric acid etch + silane), producing a durable,
  well-sealed, esthetic repair; GIC bonds chemically to dentin and metal but
  does not bond reliably to a silica- or zirconia-based ceramic surface. [6-7]

## Where GIC does have a legitimate role

- **As a base or intraorifice barrier under composite.** GIC's fluoride
  release, dentin bonding, and biocompatibility make it a reasonable deep layer
  over the obturation/orifice, sealed by an overlying bonded composite that
  carries the occlusal load and bonds to the ceramic. In an in vitro study of
  molars, conventional GIC (Fuji IX) performed well as a base material for
  certain conservative access designs, though composite-based restorations
  generally provided higher fracture resistance. [2-3][8]
- **As a provisional/interim restoration** between endodontic visits, where
  resin-reinforced GIC gives acceptable seal and retention — but this is
  explicitly a temporary indication. [4][9]

## Practical caveats

- Crown retention is reduced by the access preparation itself. Restoring the
  access with composite (or amalgam) has been shown to restore, and even
  exceed, original crown retention. [10]
- Resin-matrix ceramic (hybrid) crowns repair more predictably with composite
  than do lithium disilicate or feldspathic crowns, which chip more at the
  cavosurface margin during access. [11]
- If esthetics and load are not concerns and a bonded composite cannot be
  reliably placed, resin-modified GIC (RMGIC) is mechanically superior to
  conventional GIC, but still falls short of composite for a definitive
  load-bearing surface restoration. [4]
