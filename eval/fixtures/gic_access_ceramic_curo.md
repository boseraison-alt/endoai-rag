## Clinical recommendation — Level I evidence

Assumed: the question concerns definitive coronal seal of an endodontic access
cavity cut through an existing intact ceramic crown in an adult permanent
tooth. The retrieved evidence base does not directly address this scenario —
all Level I evidence returned concerns restorative materials in primary
dentition and pulpotomy dressings, not access cavities through indirect ceramic
restorations in endodontically treated permanent teeth. Any recommendation is
therefore extrapolated and provisional.

From the wider literature (which this search did not return): glass ionomer
cement (GIC) alone is generally not recommended as the definitive access
restoration through a ceramic crown after root canal treatment. Standard-of-care
guidance from the ESE and AAE, and the bulk of the endodontic outcomes
literature (Ray & Trope, Gillen et al., Ng et al.), supports a bonded composite
resin seal — typically over a base — because composite provides superior wear
resistance, marginal seal against occlusal loading, and better bond to the
etched ceramic (after HF + silane) than GIC. GIC is appropriate as an
intermediate/orifice seal or as a base beneath composite, but as a stand-alone
occlusal-loaded restoration it has higher wear, higher fracture rates, and
inferior marginal adaptation at the ceramic interface. The pragmatic protocol
is: sandblast/HF-etch the ceramic margins, silane, bonding agent, composite
resin (± flowable liner or GIC base over the pulp chamber floor / orifices).

Returning to what this evidence base does support: the retrieved systematic
review of restorative materials, although in primary teeth, reports annual
failure rates for GIC of 7.6–16.6% versus composite resin 0–19.5%, with
secondary caries and loss of retention as dominant failure modes for GIC
[PMID 35819627] — a pattern consistent with the concern about using GIC alone
as a load-bearing definitive seal. No retrieved study specifically validates
GIC as a definitive access-cavity restoration through a ceramic crown.

This recommendation is synthesized from published abstracts, not full texts.

## Evidence summary

### Level I — RCTs and Systematic Reviews

The EAPD systematic review by Amend et al. (2022) synthesised 29 RCTs of
restorative materials in primary teeth and reported annual failure rates of
7.6–16.6% for conventional GIC, 1.9–16.9% for resin-modified GIC, 2.9–25.6% for
high-viscosity GIC, and 0–19.5% for composite resin, with heterogeneity
precluding meta-analysis and most studies at high risk of bias [PMID 35819627].
Failure modes for GIC-family materials clustered around secondary caries, poor
marginal adaptation, and loss of retention — the same failure modes that would
be clinically relevant at a composite–ceramic access margin under occlusal load.

Abdelwahab et al. (2024) compared MTA/bioceramic pulpotomy with either GIC or a
stainless-steel crown as the final restoration and found that GIC (GC EQUIA
Forte HT) as the final restorative was significantly associated with increased
failure risk versus a full-coverage crown at 12 months [PMID 38281619].

Neither trial addresses access cavities through ceramic crowns in permanent
teeth, so both are indirect evidence only; they nonetheless converge on the
signal that GIC as a stand-alone definitive occlusal restoration underperforms
alternatives.

### Levels II–V

No retrieved studies at these levels directly addressed permanent access
restoration through ceramic crowns. This is a genuine gap in the retrieved
evidence base.
