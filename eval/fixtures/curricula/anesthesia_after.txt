# Anesthesia for Endodontics: Techniques, Selection, and Contemporary Agents
### A 20-Minute Clinical Teaching Curriculum

---

## OVERVIEW

Achieving profound, reliable pulpal anesthesia is the rate-limiting step of every endodontic procedure. Inflammatory pulpal pathophysiology — not drug ignorance — is the primary reason standard inferior alveolar nerve blocks fail in up to 72% of symptomatic mandibular molars [[PMID:26831048]], and the clinical consequence ranges from patient distress to procedure abandonment. This curriculum equips the clinician with an evidence-anchored, step-by-step framework: understanding *why* anesthesia fails (Module 1), confirming *whether* it has succeeded before the first file enters the canal (Module 2), selecting the right injection technique and agent *for the specific patient* (Module 3), and managing complications and resistance patterns when the standard algorithm is exhausted (Module 4). By the end of this 20-minute session, you will be able to apply an operational decision rule at chairside, cite the supporting evidence tier for each branch, and identify the specific gaps where current evidence remains insufficient.

---

## Module 1 — Anesthetic Mechanisms and Endodontic Anatomy

**Why anesthesia fails in endodontics — the inflammatory basis**

The central challenge of endodontic anesthesia is not pharmacological ignorance; it is pulpal pathophysiology working directly against the clinician. Chronic inflammation in irreversible pulpitis drives peripheral sensitization of nociceptors, producing persistent hyperalgesia that fundamentally alters the threshold at which local anesthetics can silence C-fibers and Aδ-fibers. Alajlan et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (n=917) using GRADE methodology and found that this sensitized state creates a measurable, quantifiable anesthesia success problem specifically for inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) in irreversible pulpitis [[PMID:38490300]]. The inflammatory milieu lowers tissue pH periapically — a mechanism the retrieved evidence base does not directly quantify in any included paper — reducing neuronal penetration at the exact site where anesthesia is most needed.

**The articaine vs. lidocaine question for posterior endodontics**

The Cochrane review by St George et al. (2018) — a systematic review and meta-analysis of 123 RCTs (n=19,223), with an industry conflict declared by Dentsply (evidence scores reduced accordingly) — reported that **4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine may be superior to 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine** for achieving pulpal anesthesia in posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis requiring root canal treatment (31% success with 2% lidocaine vs. 49% with 4% articaine; RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.10–2.32; 4 parallel studies, 203 participants; low-quality evidence) [[PMID:29990391]].

Lidocaine resulted in slightly less post-injection pain than articaine (MD 6.41 mm on a 170-mm Heft-Parker VAS, 95% CI 1.01–11.80; 3 cross-over studies, 309 interventions; moderate-quality evidence) [[PMID:29990391]].

**Corticosteroids as a pharmacological adjunct**

Preemptive systemic corticosteroids represent the most evidence-supported adjunct for improving IANB success in irreversible pulpitis. Alajlan et al. (2024), pooling 12 RCTs using random-effects inverse variance meta-analysis (n=917), found a risk ratio of 1.66 (95% CI 1.34–2.06; P<0.00001) in favor of corticosteroid premedication, rated as moderate-certainty evidence by GRADE [[PMID:38490300]]. Significant heterogeneity was present in the pooled data (I²=57%, P=0.007) [[PMID:38490300]], indicating that effect size varies across included populations and corticosteroid protocols. The specific agent and dose were not standardized across trials, and Alajlan et al. (2024) did not report a single recommended corticosteroid formulation [[PMID:38490300]].

**Supplemental intraligamentary anesthesia — and the NSAID approach**

When block anesthesia fails, supplemental techniques become essential. A pre-registered double-blind RCT by Kothari et al. (2025) comparing intraligamentary injections of 2% lignocaine, diclofenac sodium, and ketorolac tromethamine in 198 patients (99 hypertensive, 99 normotensive) with moderate-to-severe symptomatic irreversible pulpitis found that both NSAID-based intraligamentary injections produced statistically significantly lower intraoperative VAS pain scores than lignocaine (p<0.05), with no adverse effects in either group [[PMID:40705444]]. Kothari et al. (2025) did not report the specific volume used for the intraligamentary injection [[PMID:40705444]]. Notably, hypertensive patients received 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine *without* adrenaline for the primary IANB, while normotensive patients received 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine *with* adrenaline — a clinically important stratification for medical management [[PMID:40705444]].

**Anatomical variables that directly determine block success**

Standard IANB targets the mandibular foramen, but anatomical variation can silently undermine every technique. Yu et al. (2024) described an ectopic dental canal branching from the superior border of the mandibular canal that anastomosed with the mesial lingual root canal of a mandibular second molar — an accessory innervation source invisible on periapical radiographs and only identified on CBCT [[PMID:39342989]]. This recommendation rests on a single case report (Yu et al. 2024 [[PMID:39342989]]) — no RCT or prospective cohort has evaluated the prevalence or anesthetic management implications of this variant; treat as provisional pending higher-tier confirmation.

For the **maxillary second molar**, the posterior superior alveolar (PSA) block is the foundational technique. Aboytes et al. (2024), in a prospective study of 167 participants, demonstrated that reduced needle penetration depths of 10 mm (93% success) and 5 mm (92% success) achieved statistically equivalent pulpal anesthesia to the traditional 16 mm depth (85% success), using a 21 mm short 27-gauge needle, with no adverse events at any depth [[PMID:39406486]]. No statistically significant difference in success rate was found across the three depths [[PMID:39406486]].

**Antibiotics do not substitute for anesthesia in managing pain**

A Cochrane review by Cope et al. (2024) — three RCTs, 134 participants — found no difference in participant-reported pain or swelling at 24, 48, or 72 hours when systemic antibiotics (clindamycin or phenoxymethylpenicillin) were added to chemo-mechanical endodontic debridement and analgesics, for either symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess [[PMID:38712714]]. The certainty of evidence was low to very low. Effective local anesthesia enabling operative source-removal remains the only evidence-based first-line pain intervention for these conditions [[PMID:38712714]].

## Clinical Application



### 4a. Procedural Protocol

1. **Establish pulpal diagnosis before selecting anesthetic technique.** Record presence/absence of spontaneous pain and classify preoperative VAS pain as above or below 5/10 — scores ≥5/10 are associated with significantly higher odds of failure (OR 0.2, 95% CI 0.04–0.8, P=0.024 for pulp survival in symptomatic teeth) [[PMID:39442480]]. Confirm irreversible pulpitis diagnosis before escalating anesthetic plan [[PMID:38490300]].

2. **Administer preemptive systemic corticosteroid in confirmed irreversible pulpitis.** Moderate-certainty GRADE evidence (RR 1.66, 95% CI 1.34–2.06; n=917) supports corticosteroid premedication to improve IANB success [[PMID:38490300]]. The specific corticosteroid agent and dose were not standardized across the included trials and are not reported by Alajlan et al. (2024) [[PMID:38490300]]; follow local prescribing guidelines for agent selection.

3. **Select local anesthetic formulation based on pulpal status and medical history.** For mandibular posterior irreversible pulpitis in normotensive patients: 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (low-quality evidence, RR 1.60 for success vs. 2% lidocaine) [[PMID:29990391]]. For hypertensive patients: 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine *without* adrenaline for the primary IANB [[PMID:40705444]].

### 4b. Decision Tree

**IF** the tooth has confirmed irreversible pulpitis with preoperative VAS ≥5/10
**THEN** plan for primary IANB *plus* preemptive systemic corticosteroid *plus* ready supplemental intraligamentary injection
**BECAUSE** moderate-certainty meta-analysis (n=917) shows corticosteroid premedication raises IANB success RR to 1.66; preoperative pain ≥5/10 is an independent predictor of worse outcomes [[PMID:38490300]] [[PMID:39442480]]

**IF** the patient is hypertensive and requires mandibular IANB
**THEN** use 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine *without* adrenaline for the primary block
**BECAUSE** Kothari et al. (2025) specifically stratified hypertensive patients to the epinephrine-free formulation as the primary IANB in their pre-registered RCT [[PMID:40705444]]

**IF** standard primary IANB fails to achieve two consecutive negative cold-test responses
**THEN** administer supplemental intraligamentary injection with diclofenac sodium or ketorolac tromethamine rather than additional lignocaine
**BECAUSE** NSAID-based intraligamentary injections produced statistically lower intraoperative VAS pain scores than lignocaine intraligamentary (p<0.05; n=198) in both patient groups [[PMID:40705444]]

**IF** the tooth is a mandibular second molar and anesthesia fails despite IANB and supplemental injection
**THEN** obtain small-FOV CBCT to evaluate for bifid mandibular canal or ectopic dental canal anastomosing with the root canal system before re-attempting anesthesia
**BECAUSE** Yu et al. (2024) documented an ectopic canal creating accessory innervation invisible on periapical radiography [[PMID:39342989]] — this rests on a single case report; treat as provisional pending cohort-level prevalence data

**IF** the clinician considers prescribing systemic antibiotics to control pain from symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess
**THEN** proceed with operative debridement under local anesthesia *without* antibiotics unless systemic spread (cellulitis, lymphadenopathy, fever, malaise) is clinically present
**BECAUSE** Cochrane review (3 RCTs, n=134) found no reduction in pain or swelling with antibiotic adjuncts at any time point assessed [[PMID:38712714]]

## Module 2 — Clinical Assessment of Anesthetic Adequacy

**The central clinical problem** in endodontic anesthesia is not drug choice — it is knowing whether anesthesia has actually worked before you begin instrumentation. Inadequate anesthesia in irreversible pulpitis is the leading cause of intraoperative pain and procedure abandonment, and the pathophysiology is specific: chronic pulpal inflammation drives peripheral sensitization and lowered nociceptor activation thresholds, creating persistent hyperalgesia that standard inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) doses routinely fail to overcome [[PMID:38490300]].

**Why assessment matters before the first file enters the canal.** A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (n = 917) by Alajlan et al., 2024 demonstrated that the corticosteroid group achieved successful anesthesia at a risk ratio of 1.66 (95% CI 1.34–2.06, p < 0.00001), confirming — at moderate-certainty GRADE evidence — that the baseline IANB failure rate in irreversible pulpitis is high enough to justify preemptive pharmacological adjuncts [[PMID:38490300]]. This is the highest-tier evidence in the retrieved base addressing anesthetic success rates in endodontics, and all clinical decisions in this module lead from it.

**Defining anesthetic success.** The clinical benchmark is no pain or mild pain only on access and initial pulpal instrumentation. The pre-treatment pain NRS scores reported in pulpotomy trials confirm that baseline pain ≥ 5/10 is an independent predictor of procedural failure: Taha et al., 2024 found an odds ratio of 0.2 (95% CI 0.04–0.8, p = 0.024) for success when preoperative pain exceeded 5/10, underlining that high baseline pain directly predicts anesthetic difficulty [[PMID:39442480]].

**Pulpal diagnosis drives technique selection.** Van Nieuwenhuysen et al., 2023 (n = 2500, 20-year cohort) identified pre-operative pain as a significant predictor of treatment failure (HR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.23–1.97) and persistent post-operative pain as an even stronger predictor (HR = 2.63, 95% CI 1.44–4.80) [[PMID:36683563]]. Translating this: a tooth that remains painful after your block carries elevated biological risk, not merely an anesthesia inconvenience.

**Key pre-treatment adequacy indicators** (assess all before proceeding):

- Lip numbness confirmed by patient report (necessary but not sufficient for pulpal anesthesia) — standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base
- No sharp pain to cold stimulus applied to an adjacent non-target tooth (confirms block spread) — standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base
- Probing of buccal sulcus: no withdrawal response — standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base
- Percussion sensitivity: note baseline score on NRS 0–10 — standard practice, not from the retrieved evidence base
- Patient-reported spontaneous pain: baseline NRS documented; score ≥ 5/10 flags high-risk case [[PMID:39442480]]
- Duration of lingering thermal pain pre-operatively: longer preoperative pain duration correlates with worse VPT prognosis [[PMID:38701294]]

**Hemostasis as an intraoperative adequacy surrogate.** When vital pulp therapy is performed, pulpal bleeding behavior after a 2.5% sodium hypochlorite pellet application provides the most direct intraoperative signal of biological pulpal status. Taha & Albakri, 2024 (n = 200, RCT) established that hemostasis within 4 minutes in partial pulpotomy is the cutoff beyond which further tissue removal is indicated, and that increased time to hemostasis significantly predicted decreased odds of success [[PMID:38583758]]. Coll et al., 2025 extends this, concluding that hemostasis within 6 minutes likely improves pulpotomy success more broadly [[PMID:40533920]]. These thresholds — 4 minutes for the partial pulpotomy escalation decision, 6 minutes for general pulpotomy — are the only hemostasis cutoffs supported by RCT-level evidence in this evidence base.

**Corticosteroid premedication — evidence and limits.** The 2024 meta-analysis (moderate-certainty, n = 917) supports systemic corticosteroids as significantly improving IANB success (RR 1.66) [[PMID:38490300]]; however, heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 57%, p = 0.007) [[PMID:38490300]]. The retrieved abstract does not report a specific corticosteroid agent or dose regimen — dose selection should follow local prescribing guidelines, not this evidence base. This finding should be treated as a supported adjunct strategy rather than a replacement for technique refinement.

**Post-treatment pain is not an anesthesia failure — but must be pre-empted.** Mergoni et al., 2022 (Cochrane, n = 5693, moderate-certainty) found higher pain within one week in single-visit RCT groups (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.14–2.09), particularly in vital teeth (RR 2.16, 95% CI 1.39–3.36) [[PMID:36512807]]. This moderate-certainty finding means patients who experience post-operative pain in the first 7 days should not conclude that intraoperative anesthesia was inadequate [[PMID:36512807]].

### 4b. Decision Tree

**IF** baseline NRS pain score is ≥ 5/10 before injection
**THEN** plan supplemental anesthesia from the outset AND consider systemic corticosteroid premedication
**BECAUSE** preoperative pain ≥ 5/10 significantly predicts procedural failure (OR 0.2, p = 0.024) and corticosteroids improve IANB success (RR 1.66) [[PMID:39442480]] [[PMID:38490300]]

**IF** lip is numb but cold test on target tooth produces a sharp response
**THEN** do NOT proceed to access; administer supplemental injection and retest before instrumentation
**BECAUSE** lip numbness does not predict pulpal anesthesia in irreversible pulpitis; the anesthetic success literature demonstrates high IANB failure rates at pulpal level in this diagnosis [[PMID:38490300]]

**IF** pulpal access achieved and bleeding ceases within 4 minutes on 2.5% NaOCl pellet application
**THEN** proceed with partial or full pulpotomy as planned
**BECAUSE** hemostasis within 4 minutes is the RCT-derived cutoff for adequate pulpal zone health in partial pulpotomy [[PMID:38583758]]

**IF** pulpal bleeding does not cease by 4 minutes (partial pulpotomy) or 6 minutes (full pulpotomy) on 2.5% NaOCl pellet
**THEN** escalate procedure depth (partial → full pulpotomy; full pulpotomy → root canal treatment consideration)
**BECAUSE** prolonged hemostasis time significantly predicts decreased success odds [[PMID:38583758]] [[PMID:40533920]]

**IF** patient is fully anesthetized intraoperatively but reports NRS > 4/10 within 7 days post-operatively
**THEN** reassure, maintain analgesic cover, and review at 1 week; do not conclude treatment failure based on pain alone
**BECAUSE** moderate-certainty Cochrane evidence confirms higher 7-day pain incidence in single-visit vital tooth treatment without difference in radiological failure rates at 1 year [[PMID:36512807]]

## Module 3 — Injection Techniques and Contemporary Anesthetic Agents

**The Mandibular Anesthesia Problem**

Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP) creates a hostile neurochemical environment: inflamed pulpal tissue lowers local pH, reduces the unionized fraction of anesthetic available to cross nerve membranes, and upregulates tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels. The clinical consequence is a primary IANB failure rate that reaches 72% for mandibular first molars — only 28% of first molars and 25% of second molars achieve pain-free treatment with 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine alone [[PMID:26831048]]. This is the central problem of endodontic anesthesia.

**Primary Injection Strategy: IANB vs. Buccal Infiltration**

Two systematic reviews with meta-analyses now converge on a critical finding: 4% articaine buccal infiltration (BI) produces success rates statistically equivalent to 2% lidocaine IANB for mandibular first molars with SIP (RR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.93–1.20; I² = 24.51%; moderate certainty of evidence) [[PMID:39982504]]. A five-RCT meta-analysis (n = 500) confirmed no significant difference in pooled success rate [RD = 0.14 (95% CI −0.01 to 0.29); p =.08] [[PMID:35718426]]. This positions 4% articaine BI as a legitimate primary technique, particularly useful when the IANB landmark is uncertain or when the clinician wants to avoid bilateral lip numbness.

For **maxillary molars**, buccal-only infiltration is insufficient. An RCT (n = 80) showed that adding a palatal infiltration of 0.5 mL 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine to buccal infiltration significantly reduced pain during access of palatal canals compared with buccal infiltration alone (p < 0.05), regardless of whether articaine or lidocaine was used buccally [[PMID:39923048]].

**Supplemental Techniques When Primary Injection Fails**

A network meta-analysis of 46 RCTs (37 interventions) ranked supplemental intraosseous (IO) injection of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine as the highest-efficacy adjunct (SUCRA = 93%), with IO 4% articaine also near the top (SUCRA = 87%) [[PMID:31601433]]. A randomized clinical trial confirmed that supplemental IO injection (84% success) and 4% articaine buccal infiltration (84% success) outperformed repeat IANB (32%) and intraligamentary injection (48%) for pain-free treatment in mandibular teeth with SIP [[PMID:22414822]].

**The literature is currently divided on this topic** regarding intraligamentary injection (ILI) performance. Kanaa et al. [[PMID:22414822]] reported only 48% success for ILI with 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine. However, Lin et al. [[PMID:27558847]] showed a 92.1% success rate using a four-site ILI technique (mesio- and disto-buccal, followed by mesio- and disto-lingual if pain persisted) in asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis. Critically, the Lin cohort involved *asymptomatic* teeth — the conflicting results likely reflect case selection rather than technique variation. Current evidence favours IO or 4% articaine BI as supplemental techniques for *symptomatic* cases.

Intrapulpal injection, when ILI fails, achieves 100% success (45/45 cases) but is associated with elevated postoperative pain scores that persist up to 24 hours before returning to control levels by day 3 [[PMID:38768707]].

**Contemporary Agents and Adjuncts**

For supplemental ILI, NSAIDs outperform lidocaine: ketorolac tromethamine and diclofenac sodium intraligamentary injections both reduced intraoperative pain significantly more than 2% lidocaine ILI (p < 0.05) in both normotensive and hypertensive patients with SIP [[PMID:40705444]]. The hypertensive group received 1.8 mL 2% lidocaine *without* adrenaline for the primary IANB, an important safety distinction [[PMID:40705444]]. Adding tramadol hydrochloride (50 mg/mL) to 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine in a 1.2 mL dose (0.6 mL per root) as supplemental ILI produced significantly higher success than either agent alone [[PMID:38219030]].

**Premedication** amplifies primary injection efficacy. Ibuprofen sustained-release 800 mg, administered 1 hour before 3.6 mL articaine BI, raised anesthetic success from 46.7% (placebo) to 73.3% (p < 0.05) and reduced postoperative pain at 6 and 48 hours [[PMID:38528685]]. The network meta-analysis confirmed that combining preoperative NSAIDs with IANB raises the odds ratio of success to 46 (95% CrI 8–420; SUCRA = 94%) [[PMID:31601433]].

**Delivery Systems and Special Populations**

Computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery (CCLAD) — tested with the SOGA Smart II device using 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine — reduced injection pain VAS from 4.20 ± 1.16 (conventional) to 2.23 ± 1.23 (p < 0.001), with the majority of patients preferring the electronic system [[PMID:41703537]].

In pediatric patients where epinephrine is undesirable, 3% mepivacaine plain buccal infiltration provided effective anesthesia with significantly lower heart rate elevation at 5 minutes post-administration (p < 0.0001) and lower postoperative pain scores (p = 0.0046) compared with 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline in children aged 6–13 years [[PMID:41026565]]. Cryotherapy (ice pack applied to buccal vestibule for 5 minutes before IANB) raised IANB success from 40.8% to 59.2% in adolescents receiving 3.6 mL 4% articaine (p = 0.004) and reduced the need for supplemental ILI from 67.1% to 50% (p = 0.032) [[PMID:37268843]].

## Clinical Application

### 4a. Procedural Protocol

1. **Premedicate with ibuprofen SR 800 mg** — Administer 1 hour before the appointment for patients with SIP in a mandibular molar. This raises articaine BI success from 46.7% to 73.3% [[PMID:38528685]]. Contraindicated in patients with NSAID hypersensitivity, active peptic ulcer, or renal impairment — omit and proceed to step 2.

2. **Administer primary injection** — Normotensive patients: 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine via IANB, or 3.6 mL of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine via buccal infiltration (equivalent success rates) [[PMID:39982504]] [[PMID:35718426]]. Hypertensive patients: 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine *without* adrenaline for IANB [[PMID:40705444]].

3. **Wait 20 minutes before beginning access** — Elheeny et al. mandated 20-minute onset time for all patients after IANB with 3.6 mL 4% articaine [[PMID:37268843]]. Confirm lip numbness before proceeding.

4. **Add palatal infiltration for maxillary molars** — Inject 0.5 mL of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine palatally in addition to buccal injection to ensure coverage of palatal canals [[PMID:39923048]].

5. **Assess intraoperative pain using HP-VAS** — HP-VAS score ≤54 mm = successful anesthesia; >54 mm = primary failure requiring supplemental injection [[PMID:38768707]] [[PMID:40321815]].

6. **If primary injection fails — first supplemental step** — Administer supplemental IO injection of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine, or supplemental 4% articaine buccal infiltration (for molars: 42–48% success; for premolars: 73% success) [[PMID:26831048]] [[PMID:22414822]].

7. **If IO/BI fails — second supplemental step (ILI)** — Inject 1.2 mL total (0.6 mL mesial root + 0.6 mL distal root) of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine plus tramadol hydrochloride 50 mg/mL for maximum ILI efficacy [[PMID:38219030]]. Alternatively, use ketorolac tromethamine or diclofenac sodium ILI in hypertensive patients to avoid vasoconstrictor [[PMID:40705444]]. Warn patients of elevated postoperative pain for up to 24 hours post-ILI [[PMID:38768707]].

8. **Last resort — intrapulpal injection** — If ILI fails, inject 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine directly into the pulp chamber under back-pressure. Success rate is 100% (45/45 cases); inform patient of likely post-treatment soreness resolving by day 3 [[PMID:38768707]].

9. **Pediatric modification** — For children aged 6–13 years where epinephrine is undesirable, substitute 3% mepivacaine plain via buccal infiltration. Apply ice pack cryotherapy to buccal vestibule for 5 minutes before injection to raise primary success rate [[PMID:37268843]] [[PMID:41026565]].

10. **Consider CCLAD delivery** — Use computer-controlled delivery (e.g., SOGA Smart II) with 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for anxious or needle-phobic patients; reduces injection VAS from 4.20 to 2.23 [[PMID:41703537]].

## Module 4 — Complications, Resistance Patterns, and Alternative Anesthetic Strategies

**Postoperative Pain: The Core Complication**

The highest-tier evidence on endodontic complications comes from a Cochrane review of 47 RCTs (5,693 teeth, up to 120-month follow-up) [[PMID:36512807]]. Single-visit root canal treatment produced a significantly higher proportion of patients reporting pain within one week compared with multiple-visit treatment (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.14–2.09; 5 studies, 638 teeth; moderate-certainty evidence) [[PMID:36512807]]. This effect was amplified in vital teeth (RR 2.16, 95% CI 1.39–3.36) and when mechanical instrumentation was used (RR 1.80, 95% CI 1.10–2.92) [[PMID:36512807]]. Critically, no difference in radiological failure was found between regimens (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.81–1.07; moderate-certainty evidence), and analgesic consumption did not differ significantly [[PMID:36512807]]. The clinical implication: neither approach prevents complications — pain management expectations must be set for every patient regardless of visit number.

A pre-registered RCT (n=120) found that across four sealer types — three calcium silicate-based (NeoSealer Flo, MTA-Bioseal, GuttaFlow Bioseal) and one epoxy resin-based (AH Plus) — VAS pain scores peaked at 6 hours and decreased significantly by 48 hours for all sealers, with no significant differences between sealer types [[PMID:39523312]]. Vital pulp status and female sex were associated with greater analgesic intake within the first 24 hours [[PMID:39523312]].

**Anesthesia Resistance: The Irreversible Pulpitis Problem**

The central challenge for anesthesia in endodontics is irreversible pulpitis, where chronic inflammation sensitizes nociceptors, rendering standard inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) insufficient. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (917 participants) provides the highest-tier evidence: preemptive systemic corticosteroids significantly improved anesthesia success for IANB in irreversible pulpitis cases (risk ratio = 1.66, 95% CI 1.34–2.06; P <.00001; moderate-certainty evidence; I² = 57%) [[PMID:38490300]]. This represents a meaningful adjunct when standard IANB is anticipated to fail.

**Antibiotic Adjuncts: Restrain Use to Specific Indications**

**The literature is currently divided on this topic.** The Cochrane review by Cope et al., 2024 [[PMID:38712714]] (3 RCTs, 134 participants) found that preoperative clindamycin produced little to no difference in pain (median NRS 3.0 vs. 3.0 at 24 hours, P = 0.219) or swelling (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.10–2.56) when added to endodontic debridement for symptomatic apical periodontitis (low-certainty evidence) [[PMID:38712714]]. A seven-day course of phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin VK) likewise showed no benefit over placebo for acute apical abscess with debridement (very low-certainty evidence) [[PMID:38712714]]. Current guidelines recommend antibiotics only for spreading infections (cellulitis, lymph node involvement, diffuse swelling) or systemic involvement (fever, malaise) — the evidence does not support their routine use for localized endodontic pain [[PMID:38712714]].

**Predictors of Treatment Failure and Resistance to Healing**

A 25-year retrospective cohort (2,500 treatments) identified pre-operative pain (HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.23–1.97) and persistent pain (HR 2.63, 95% CI 1.44–4.80) as significant independent predictors of treatment failure [[PMID:36683563]]. Periapical lesion size was also independently predictive (HR 1.88, 95% CI 1.67–2.11); larger lesions require longer healing timelines [[PMID:36683563]]. A retrospective cohort of 1,262 patients found untreated additional canals present in 21.7% of all failure cases, and in 36.9% of short-term failures (<5 years) — the single strongest predictor of early failure [[PMID:38475776]].

**Alternative Strategies: Posterior Superior Alveolar Block Modifications**

A prospective study (167 participants) compared PSA block needle penetration depths of 16 mm, 10 mm, and 5 mm using a 27-gauge short (21 mm) needle [[PMID:39406486]]. Success rates were 85%, 93%, and 92% respectively — not statistically different — with no adverse events at any depth [[PMID:39406486]]. This recommendation rests on a single prospective study of 167 participants (Aboytes et al., 2024 [[PMID:39406486]]) — no RCT or systematic review has yet replicated these findings; treat as provisional pending higher-tier confirmation.

### 4b. Decision Tree

**IF** the patient has confirmed irreversible pulpitis and cold testing remains positive after standard IANB
**THEN** administer preemptive systemic corticosteroid before repeating IANB
**BECAUSE** corticosteroid premedication significantly improves IANB success in irreversible pulpitis (RR 1.66, 95% CI 1.34–2.06; moderate-certainty evidence) [[PMID:38490300]]

**IF** maxillary posterior anesthesia is required and hematoma risk from traditional 16 mm PSA block depth is a concern
**THEN** use a 27-gauge short (21 mm) needle at 10 mm penetration depth
**BECAUSE** 10 mm depth achieved 93% pulpal anesthesia success for the maxillary second molar with no adverse events; statistically equivalent to 16 mm [[PMID:39406486]]

**IF** the patient presents with symptomatic apical periodontitis or localized acute apical abscess with no evidence of spreading infection, cellulitis, or systemic involvement
**THEN** proceed with operative endodontic debridement WITHOUT systemic antibiotics
**BECAUSE** Cochrane evidence (3 RCTs, 134 participants) shows no benefit of clindamycin or penicillin VK on pain or swelling when debridement is performed [[PMID:38712714]]

**IF** a vital tooth is planned for single-visit RCT with mechanical instrumentation
**THEN** document informed consent for elevated one-week pain probability and prescribe scheduled analgesics for 48 hours
**BECAUSE** this subgroup carries the highest post-obturation pain risk (RR 1.80 vs. multi-visit, mechanical instrumentation subgroup; moderate-certainty) [[PMID:36512807]]

**IF** early endodontic failure (<5 years post-treatment) is identified radiographically
**THEN** prioritize CBCT evaluation for untreated additional canals before planning retreatment
**BECAUSE** missed canals were present in 36.9% of short-term failure cases, the leading cause of early failure [[PMID:38475776]]

**IF** periapical lesion size is ≥10 mm or a sinus tract is present
**THEN** plan multi-visit treatment with intracanal medicament and counsel patient on extended healing timeline
**BECAUSE** lesion size independently predicts failure (HR 1.88, 95% CI 1.67–2.11), and prior abscess/sinus tract worsen prognosis [[PMID:36683563]] [[PMID:39151889]]

## KEY TAKEAWAYS

**1. Never skip preoperative pain scoring — it is a triage instrument, not a formality.**
A preoperative NRS ≥5/10 carries an odds ratio of 0.2 for treatment success [[PMID:39442480]] and independently predicts anesthesia failure at block level [[PMID:38490300]]. This single number should trigger your full corticosteroid-plus-supplemental-injection protocol before you load the syringe, not after the first file causes pain.

**2. Preemptive systemic corticosteroids are the strongest adjunct supported by meta-analytic evidence.**
A 12-RCT meta-analysis (n=917) documents a risk ratio of 1.66 (95% CI 1.34–2.06) for IANB success with corticosteroid premedication at moderate GRADE certainty [[PMID:38490300]]. Because the specific agent and dose were not standardized across included trials, follow your local formulary — but commit to prescribing an adjunct rather than defaulting to re-injection alone.

**3. Intraosseous injection is your highest-ranked rescue when primary block fails — NSAID intraligamentary injection is the preferred supplemental route in hypertensive patients.**
Network meta-analysis of 46 RCTs places supplemental IO injection at SUCRA 93% for anesthesia success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis [[PMID:31601433]]. Where IO is unavailable or the patient is hypertensive, ketorolac tromethamine or diclofenac sodium intraligamentary injection outperform 2% lidocaine intraligamentary injection (p<0.05, n=198) with no cardiovascular adverse effects [[PMID:40705444]].

**4. Postoperative pain after single-visit vital pulp treatment is a biological expectation, not a clinical error — counsel before, not after.**
Moderate-certainty Cochrane evidence (n=5,693) shows that single-visit vital tooth RCT carries RR 2.16 for pain in the first 7 days compared with multi-visit [[PMID:36512807]]. Pre-emptively prescribe scheduled analgesia for 48 hours, prioritize vital cases and female patients, and set this expectation in writing before dismissal.

**5. Emerging: Ibuprofen SR 800 mg one hour before injection and cryotherapy five minutes before IANB can meaningfully boost primary success rates.**
Ibuprofen SR 800 mg pre-procedure raised 4% articaine buccal-infiltration success from 46.7% to 73.3% [[PMID:38528685]]; ice-pack cryotherapy raised IANB success from 40.8% to 59.2% in adolescents [[PMID:37268843]]. Both findings rest on single RCTs with limited follow-up and require replication, but the intervention cost and risk are negligible and the effect sizes are clinically meaningful.

## The Final Verdict

### 1. Decision Rule

**Scenario A — Mandibular posterior tooth, confirmed irreversible pulpitis, normotensive adult, NRS ≥5/10:**
→ Prescribe ibuprofen SR 800 mg orally 1 hour pre-appointment AND systemic corticosteroid per local formulary → Deliver primary IANB with 1.8 mL 2% lidocaine 1:100,000 epinephrine OR 4% articaine 3.6 mL buccal infiltration → Wait 20 minutes → Cold-test: if HP-VAS >54 mm → supplemental IO injection (2% lidocaine 1:100,000 epinephrine) → If IO fails → ILI 1.2 mL (0.6 mL/root) lidocaine 1:80,000 with tramadol 50 mg/mL → If ILI fails → intrapulpal injection under backpressure (100% intraoperative success) [[PMID:38490300]] [[PMID:38528685]] [[PMID:31601433]] [[PMID:38768707]].

**Scenario B — Mandibular posterior tooth, confirmed irreversible pulpitis, hypertensive adult:**
→ Primary IANB with 1.8 mL 2% lignocaine *without* adrenaline → if block fails → supplemental ILI with ketorolac tromethamine or diclofenac sodium (NSAID ILI superior to lidocaine ILI, p<0.05, no cardiovascular adverse events) [[PMID:40705444]].

**Scenario C — Maxillary second molar, any pulpal diagnosis:**
→ PSA block with 27-gauge short (21 mm) needle at 10 mm penetration depth (93% success, no adverse events) + palatal infiltration 0.5 mL 4% articaine 1:100,000 epinephrine (significantly reduces palatal canal pain vs. buccal alone) [[PMID:39406486]] [[PMID:39923048]].

**Scenario D — Vital tooth, planned single-visit RCT with mechanical instrumentation:**
→ Execute anesthesia protocol per Scenario A or C → document informed consent for elevated 7-day pain risk → prescribe scheduled analgesic for 48 hours → multi-visit approach if patient risk-averse [[PMID:36512807]].

**Scenario E — Localized apical periodontitis/abscess, no systemic signs:**
→ Achieve local anesthesia → perform operative debridement → do NOT prescribe systemic antibiotics (no benefit at 24/48/72 h) [[PMID:38712714]].

### 2. Evidence Anchor

**Scenario A — Corticosteroid premedication for IANB in irreversible pulpitis:**
**Anchored in:** Level I systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (n=917), moderate GRADE certainty [[PMID:38490300]].

**Scenario A — Ibuprofen SR 800 mg premedication:**
**Anchored in:** Level I single RCT (n=60), statistically significant effect on articaine BI success [[PMID:38528685]]; supported by network meta-analysis (46 RCTs, SUCRA 94% for NSAID+IANB combination) [[PMID:31601433]].

**Scenario A — IO injection as highest-ranked supplemental technique:**
**Anchored in:** Level I network meta-analysis of 46 RCTs (37 interventions), SUCRA 93% [[PMID:31601433]]; confirmed by randomized clinical trial (IO 84% success vs. repeat IANB 32%) [[PMID:22414822]].

**Scenario A — Intrapulpal injection as last resort:**
**Anchored in:** Level I RCT (n=45 intrapulpal cases, 100% intraoperative success) [[PMID:38768707]].

**Scenario B — Epinephrine-free IANB + NSAID ILI for hypertensive patients:**
**Anchored in:** Level I pre-registered double-blind RCT (n=198, 99 hypertensive) [[PMID:40705444]].

**Scenario C — Reduced-depth PSA block at 10 mm:**
**Anchored in:** Level II single prospective study (n=167); no RCT or systematic review has replicated this finding — treat as Level II evidence pending multicentre confirmation [[PMID:39406486]].

**Scenario C — Palatal infiltration for maxillary molar coverage:**
**Anchored in:** Level III retrospective cohort (n=80) — no RCT yet evaluates this comparison [[PMID:39923048]].

**Scenario D — Single-visit vital tooth pain risk:**
**Anchored in:** Cochrane systematic review, 47 RCTs (n=5,693, up to 120 months follow-up), moderate GRADE certainty [[PMID:36512807]].

**Scenario E — Antibiotic non-use in localized endodontic infection:**
**Anchored in:** Cochrane systematic review, 3 RCTs (n=134), low-to-very-low certainty — larger RCTs needed to strengthen this branch [[PMID:38712714]].

### 3. Where Uncertainty Remains

- **Optimal corticosteroid agent, dose, and route for IANB augmentation:** The meta-analysis reporting RR 1.66 pooled 12 RCTs that used different corticosteroid protocols without standardization [[PMID:38490300]]; the moderate heterogeneity (I²=57%) reflects this variability. No head-to-head RCT comparing dexamethasone vs. methylprednisolone vs. betamethasone at fixed doses in irreversible pulpitis has been published. A multi-arm parallel-group RCT with standardized dosing and blinded pulpal testing would resolve which agent, dose, and route produces the effect size and safety profile suitable for routine clinical adoption.

- **Intraligamentary injection success rates across symptomatic vs. asymptomatic cases:** The current evidence base contains directly conflicting success figures — 48% (Kanaa et al., symptomatic, 2% lidocaine, RCT design) [[PMID:22414822]] vs. 92.1% (Lin et al., asymptomatic, four-site technique) [[PMID:27558847]]. No head-to-head RCT has randomized symptomatic irreversible pulpitis patients to ILI technique variants using the same agent, controlled for tooth type and operator. A multicentre RCT stratified by symptomatic/asymptomatic status and comparing single-site vs. four-site ILI with standardized agents would resolve whether the discrepancy is due to pulpal status, technique, or agent selection.

- **Prevalence and anesthetic management of ectopic dental canal anatomy causing block failure:** The only primary evidence for CBCT-identified ectopic canal as a cause of anesthesia failure in mandibular second molars is a single case report [[PMID:39342989]]. Neither the population prevalence of this variant nor the best anesthetic response (targeted infiltration vs. modified IO approach) has been studied in a cohort design. A prospective CBCT prevalence study of mandibular second molar canal anatomy in patients with repeated unexplained anesthesia failure would provide the minimum evidence needed to justify routine imaging in this scenario.

- **Long-term success of NSAID-based intraligamentary injection as supplemental anesthesia:** Kothari et al. (2025) [[PMID:40705444]] demonstrated short-term superiority of ketorolac and diclofenac ILI over lidocaine ILI for intraoperative pain, but the study measured only intraoperative VAS and reported no follow-up beyond the appointment. The pharmacological mechanism implies a local anti-inflammatory rather than anaesthetic effect; whether this affects pulp healing trajectory, post-operative pain at 24/48/72 hours, or retreatment rates is entirely unknown. A follow-up RCT with minimum 1-week postoperative pain monitoring and 12-month radiographic assessment would address this gap.

- **CCLAD delivery systems vs. conventional injection for anesthesia efficacy (not just injection comfort):** The SOGA Smart II data demonstrate reduced injection pain VAS from 4.20 to 2.23 [[PMID:41703537]], but this single RCT measured injection discomfort only — not pulpal anesthesia success rates, onset time, or cartridge volume equivalence. Whether slower computer-controlled delivery changes the pharmacokinetics of pulpal anesthesia in irreversible pulpitis remains unstudied. A prospective RCT comparing CCLAD vs. conventional IANB using identical agents, with primary endpoint of cold-test negativity at 10 and 20 minutes, would establish whether CCLAD offers an anesthetic — not merely a comfort — advantage.

---

### 4. When NOT to Apply This Rule

- **Mandibular anesthesia failure in the setting of signs of spreading infection (floor-of-mouth swelling, trismus, raised floor, difficulty swallowing or breathing):** The standard escalating injection algorithm will not reliably achieve profound anesthesia in a fascial space infection and delays the definitive airway and systemic management these patients require. → **Action:** Do not persist with repeated blocks. Initiate urgent medical referral or hospital transfer; manage airway first; antibiotic therapy is indicated for this indication specifically.

- **Persistent unexplained anesthesia failure after full protocol completion (IO + ILI + intrapulpal) in a tooth with no clear periapical pathology and atypical pain character (pain triggered by stress, not thermal/percussion, bilateral or non-dermatomal distribution):** This presentation raises the differential of non-odontogenic pain (atypical odontalgia, neurovascular, myofascial, or neuropathic pain disorder) for which endodontic intervention is contraindicated and will worsen prognosis. → **Action:** Halt treatment, document atypical pain features, refer to an orofacial pain specialist before any further endodontic intervention.

- **Complex medical co-morbidity significantly altering drug metabolism or contraindication profile (severe renal impairment precluding NSAID premedication, methaemoglobinaemia risk with articaine/prilocaine in G6PD-deficient patients, documented local anesthetic allergy requiring alternative agent selection):** The specific agent volumes and sequences in this decision rule assume standard pharmacokinetics and absence of contraindication. → **Action:** Conduct a formal pre-treatment medical consultation or specialist referral to confirm safe agent selection; do not default to escalating doses of the standard agents without pharmacological review.
