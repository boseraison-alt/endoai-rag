# Anesthesia for Endodontics: Techniques, Agents, and the Resistant Mandibular Molar
### A 20-Minute Teaching Curriculum for Adult Endodontic Practice

---

## OVERVIEW

Achieving reliable pulpal anesthesia in the symptomatic mandibular molar is one of the most evidence-tested challenges in adult endodontic practice. Standard inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) fails in the majority of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP) cases — not because of poor technique, but because inflammation fundamentally alters nerve physiology and local anesthetic pharmacodynamics [[PMID:26831048]] [[PMID:35947082]]. This curriculum equips clinicians with a layered, evidence-anchored framework: understanding *why* blocks fail, diagnosing failure early with validated metrics, selecting the right supplemental technique at the right moment, and choosing among the newest materials and delivery systems now supported by Level I evidence. By the end of this 20-minute session, the clinician will possess a decisive chairside decision tree applicable to every difficult mandibular molar encountered in adult practice.

---

## Module 1 — Anesthesia Physiology and Local Anesthetic Pharmacology

**Why Mandibular Molars Are Uniquely Resistant**

The inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) fails at clinically unacceptable rates specifically in teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP). In a pooled retrospective analysis of 204 emergency patients, standard IANB with 2% lidocaine/1:100,000 epinephrine succeeded in only 28% of mandibular first molars and 25% of second molars [[PMID:26831048]]. This is not operator error — it is pharmacophysiology. Inflamed pulp tissue generates a local acidic environment that shifts the Henderson-Hasselbalch equilibrium of local anesthetics toward the ionized, membrane-impermeant form; only the uncharged free-base form penetrates the lipid nerve membrane. Simultaneously, tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels (Nav1.8, Nav1.9) are upregulated in inflamed pulp, raising the depolarization threshold and demanding higher intracellular drug concentrations to achieve block. These two mechanisms together produce the clinical "hot tooth." The evidence base provided does not include a primary mechanistic study that quantifies the pH shift or channel upregulation numerically; the mechanism described above reflects consensus physiology not directly cited in the supplied papers.

**The Literature Is Currently Divided on This Topic — Agent Selection for IANB in SIP**

A 2020 Bayesian network meta-analysis (16 RCTs, high-quality evidence) ranked articaine first (73% success probability) and lidocaine last (12% success probability) for IANB in SIP, with a significant lidocaine-vs-articaine risk ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.63–0.88) [[PMID:31813471]]. The Cochrane review (123 RCTs, n = 19,223) independently reported that 4% articaine/1:100,000 epinephrine may be superior to 2% lidocaine/1:100,000 epinephrine for posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis (31% vs. 49% success; RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.10–2.32; low-quality evidence) [[PMID:29990391]]. Note: this Cochrane paper carries an industry-funding disclosure (Dentsply); findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Conversely, a 2025 PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis (12 studies, n = 1,262) found no statistically significant efficacy difference among 2% lidocaine, 4% articaine, 0.5% bupivacaine, and 2% mepivacaine for IANB, and concluded 2% lidocaine with epinephrine was the most cost-effective option (negative ICER) [[PMID:41075922]]. A separate network meta-analysis (11 RCTs, n = 750) ranked 2% mepivacaine with epinephrine highest by SUCRA (0.81) and found it significantly outperformed lidocaine (RR 1.42, 95% CI 1.04–1.95) [[PMID:30638269]]. The higher-tier 2025 pooled data currently favour lidocaine as a cost-effective starting point, while the Bayesian network analysis and the Cochrane review support articaine's advantage in confirmed hot-tooth scenarios. Current standard-of-care based on this evidence base: begin with 3.6 mL of 2% lidocaine/1:100,000 epinephrine and escalate agent or technique on failure [[PMID:41075922]] [[PMID:33040335]].

**Volume Matters More Than Most Clinicians Realize**

A high-quality (GRADE: high) meta-analysis of four RCTs (n = 280) showed that 3.6 mL of anesthetic for IANB produced significantly higher success than 1.8 mL (RR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.07–3.52), and trial sequential analysis confirmed this evidence as conclusive [[PMID:33040335]]. A retrospective study of 3,169 subjects confirmed that a two-cartridge volume reduced missed blocks from 7.7% to 2.3% in SIP patients (P = .0395) [[PMID:25770038]]. These two bodies of evidence together establish a minimum two-cartridge (3.6 mL) standard for IANB in any hot-tooth scenario [[PMID:33040335]] [[PMID:25770038]].

**Adjunct Pharmacology: Additives and Premedication**

A PROSPERO-registered SR (14 RCTs, n = 434 in the SIP subgroup) found that lidocaine additives including sodium bicarbonate and mannitol did not significantly increase IANB success (RR = 1.22, 95% CI 0.98–1.52; low certainty evidence), and concluded that supplemental injections remain necessary [[PMID:35398916]]. In contrast, an umbrella review of 12 systematic reviews demonstrated that NSAIDs as oral premedication — including ibuprofen, ketorolac, and diclofenac — consistently improved IANB success by blocking prostaglandin-mediated peripheral sensitization via cyclooxygenase pathways [[PMID:36988825]]. An RCT (n = 250) showed ibuprofen 600 mg, ketorolac 10 mg, meloxicam 7.5 mg, and dexamethasone 0.5 mg administered 60 minutes pre-operatively all produced significantly higher IANB success than placebo (52–64% vs. 32%; P < .05) [[PMID:36421046]]. Magnesium sulphate as an adjuvant to lidocaine also shows promise: replacing 0.2 mL of a 1.8 mL lidocaine solution with 10% magnesium sulphate raised IANB success and reduced perioperative VAS to 0.16 ± 0.37 vs. 3.13 ± 0.77 in the standard lidocaine group (P = .01) [[PMID:39213456]]. This recommendation rests on a single RCT of 86 patients (Sitharthan et al. 2024) — no multi-centre trial or systematic review has yet evaluated this approach; treat as provisional pending higher-tier confirmation.

### 4a. Procedural Protocol

1. **Administer oral NSAID premedication 60 minutes before injection.** Ibuprofen 600 mg, ketorolac 10 mg, meloxicam 7.5 mg, or dexamethasone 0.5 mg each produced significantly higher IANB success than placebo (52–64% vs. 32%; P < .05) when given 60 minutes pre-operatively [[PMID:36421046]]. NSAIDs act via cyclooxygenase-mediated prostaglandin blockade to reduce peripheral sensitization [[PMID:36988825]]. Avoid NSAIDs in patients with contraindications and document the choice made.

2. **Record pre-operative pain on a 0–170 mm Heft-Parker VAS.** This establishes baseline severity and enables standardized intraoperative comparison. The Heft-Parker VAS was used as the success metric across the key RCTs cited in this module [[PMID:36421046]] [[PMID:40321815]].

3. **Select agent and prepare two cartridges (3.6 mL total).** Default: 2% lidocaine/1:100,000 epinephrine, 3.6 mL, on cost-effectiveness grounds [[PMID:41075922]]. For a confirmed prior IANB failure with lidocaine: switch to 4% articaine/1:100,000 epinephrine, 3.6 mL, where network meta-analysis assigns the highest success probability (73%) [[PMID:31813471]].

4. **Administer conventional IANB with aspirating syringe, 25-gauge long needle.** Deposit the full 3.6 mL at the mandibular foramen. A two-cartridge volume reduced missed blocks in SIP patients from 7.7% to 2.3% (P = .0395) [[PMID:25770038]].

5. **Confirm lip numbness within 15–20 minutes.** A missed block is defined as no lip numbness by 15–20 minutes post-injection [[PMID:25770038]]. If no numbness at 20 minutes: re-administer before proceeding — do not initiate access on an unconfirmed block.

6. **Wait 20 minutes from confirmed numbness before initiating access.** This onset interval was used as the minimum threshold before endodontic procedures in the articaine RCT referenced in this module [[PMID:37268843]].

7. **Assess intraoperative VAS at dentin access and at first patency file (#10 K-file).** Success = VAS ≤ 54 mm (no or mild pain) [[PMID:26831048]]. If VAS > 54 mm at either point: stop and administer supplemental injection immediately.

8. **Supplemental intraligamentary injection (ILI) on IANB failure.** Use 4% articaine/1:100,000 epinephrine. Inject at mesiobuccal and distobuccal root aspects first (2-site technique); add mesiolingual and distolingual sites if pain persists. A 4-site ILI protocol achieved cumulative pulpal anesthesia success of 92.1% [[PMID:27558847]]. For supplemental ILI specifically, 4% articaine/1:100,000 epinephrine showed higher success than 3% plain mepivacaine (87.1% vs. 53.1%; P < .05) [[PMID:40321815]].

9. **If 4-site ILI fails: proceed to intraosseous (IO) injection.** Use 4% articaine/1:100,000 epinephrine, 1.7 mL, via a computerized delivery device. In an RCT of 64 patients with SIP, primary IO using the QuickSleeper 5th-generation device achieved 81.2% success vs. 40.6% for IANB (P < .001) [[PMID:39503145]].

10. **Document all injections, volumes, agents, and intraoperative VAS scores** before patient dismissal.

## Module 2 — Clinical Assessment of Anesthetic Failure in Mandibular Molars

**Why IANB Routinely Fails in Symptomatic Irreversible Pulpitis**

The inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) is the primary technique for mandibular molar anesthesia, yet it fails at alarming rates when pulpal inflammation is present. Habib et al. (2022) reported overall IANB success rates of only 35.8% with 2% mepivacaine and 41.2% with 4% articaine in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP), meaning the majority of patients will require supplemental intervention [[PMID:35947082]]. Preoperative pain intensity and tooth type were independently associated with intraoperative pain in that same cohort, giving you two actionable predictors at the assessment stage [[PMID:35947082]]. Missed blocks compound this problem: Fowler et al. (2015) found that a 1-cartridge volume (1.8 mL) of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine produced a missed block rate of 7.7% in SIP patients, compared to 2.3% with a 2-cartridge volume (3.6 mL) [[PMID:25770038]].

**Defining Failure Clinically**

The evidence base uniformly uses a threshold of >54 mm on the 170 mm Heft-Parker visual analogue scale (HP-VAS) to define anesthetic failure — moderate-to-severe pain at any point during access preparation or canal instrumentation [[PMID:40321815]] [[PMID:38219030]] [[PMID:28370327]]. Lip numbness at 15–20 minutes post-injection confirms IAN deposition but does not guarantee pulpal anesthesia; Fowler et al. explicitly tracked these as distinct outcomes [[PMID:25770038]]. Record both on every SIP case.

**Key Predictors of Failure — Red Flags at Assessment:**

- Moderate-to-severe preoperative pain on HP-VAS (independent predictor of intraoperative pain) [[PMID:35947082]]
- Mandibular first molar presentation (tooth type significantly associated with failure) [[PMID:35947082]]
- High dental anxiety score on Modified Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS) [[PMID:41360139]]
- Single-cartridge IANB planned (7.7% missed-block rate vs. 2.3% with two cartridges) [[PMID:25770038]]
- Patient presenting as emergency with acute SIP

**Articaine vs. Lidocaine — What the Evidence Actually Shows**

**The literature is currently divided on this topic.** The Cochrane review by St George et al. (2018) — pooling 4 parallel studies, 203 participants — found low-quality evidence that 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine was superior to 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis requiring root canal treatment (31% success with lidocaine vs. 49% with articaine; RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.10–2.32) [[PMID:29990391]]. However, Habib et al. (2022) in a larger RCT (n=330) found no statistically significant difference in IANB success between 3.6 mL 2% mepivacaine with 1:100,000 adrenalin and 3.4 mL 4% articaine with 1:100,000 adrenalin (35.8% vs. 41.2%; RR of failure 1.09, 95% CI 0.92–1.30) [[PMID:35947082]]. The larger, more recent RCT thus fails to confirm articaine superiority at the primary IANB stage; clinicians should not rely on agent selection alone to solve pulpal anesthesia failure in SIP. Note: the Cochrane review carried an industry conflict declaration (Dentsply); weight its articaine-superiority finding accordingly [[PMID:29990391]].

Volume also matters less than expected: Silva et al. (2019) found no significant difference in pulpal anesthesia between 1.8 mL and 3.6 mL of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for IANB in SIP molars (27% vs. 42%; RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.35–1.14, p=0.12), and neither volume produced reliable pain control during pulpectomy [[PMID:31365529]].

**Supplemental Strategy: The Core Principle**

The 2025 network meta-analysis by Rujirawan et al. (28 RCTs) established that both strategies — supplementing after confirmed IANB failure (SUPP; RR=2.02, 95% CI 1.55–2.30, SUCRA 85.1%) and combining supplemental injections with the primary IANB (COMB; RR=1.86, 95% CI 1.50–2.30, SUCRA 64.9%) — significantly outperformed IANB alone, with no statistically significant difference between SUPP and COMB [[PMID:40734367]]. This is the highest-tier evidence in this evidence base and anchors your clinical algorithm: plan for supplemental injection on every SIP mandibular molar case.

**Anxiety as a Modifiable Variable**

Singla et al. (2025; n=110) demonstrated that music distraction throughout the appointment produced significantly higher IANB success than either 0.5 mg etizolam taken 1 hour preoperatively or placebo (p<0.01) — an inexpensive, zero-risk intervention applicable to anxious SIP patients [[PMID:41360139]].

### 4a. Procedural Protocol

1. **Pre-screen for failure risk.** Score preoperative pain on HP-VAS; document tooth type (first vs. second molar). High preoperative pain combined with mandibular first molar = highest failure risk; plan supplemental injection from the outset [[PMID:35947082]].

2. **Offer music distraction.** Instruct the patient to select music and provide headphones before any injection is given; this intervention produced significantly higher IANB success than placebo or 0.5 mg etizolam in anxious SIP patients (p<0.01) [[PMID:41360139]].

3. **Consider preoperative dexamethasone.** Systemic dexamethasone increased IANB success (OR=2.59, 95% CI 1.46–4.59 vs. placebo) and reduced postoperative pain at 6h, 12h, and 24h in SIP patients [[PMID:35890176]]. Franco-de la Torre et al. did not specify the route or dose used across included studies; follow institutional protocol for dosing.

4. **Deliver 2-cartridge primary IANB.** Use a 2-cartridge volume to reduce the missed-block rate from 7.7% to 2.3% compared with a single cartridge in SIP patients [[PMID:25770038]]. The Cochrane review supports 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine as a primary agent, with the caveat of low-quality evidence and an industry conflict of interest declaration [[PMID:29990391]].

5. **Confirm lip numbness at 15–20 minutes.** Absence of lip numbness defines a missed block [[PMID:25770038]]. If missed, repeat IANB before proceeding to pulpal testing or access preparation.

6. **Wait full onset — minimum 10 minutes** after confirmed lip numbness before testing pulpal response. The evidence base does not specify an exact onset wait time beyond the lip-numbness confirmation window used by Fowler et al. [[PMID:25770038]].

7. **Test pulpal anesthesia before instrumentation.** Kanaa et al. (2012) used electric pulp testing to maximum stimulation (80 reading) to confirm successful pulpal anesthesia before commencing treatment [[PMID:22414822]]. Pain response = proceed to supplemental injection before access.

8. **Administer intraligamentary (PDL) supplemental injection.** Aggarwal et al. (2018) showed that 0.6 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine per root yielded 84% supplemental success vs. 64% with 0.2 mL (p=0.03) [[PMID:28370327]]. Ghosh et al. (2025) demonstrated that 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine as the PDL agent outperformed 3% plain mepivacaine (87.1% vs. 60.6% with aceclofenac premedication, p<0.05) [[PMID:40321815]]; deliver 0.6 mL per root using a pressure syringe.

9. **If PDL injection fails, escalate to articaine buccal infiltration or intraosseous injection.** Kanaa et al. (2012) found that articaine buccal infiltration achieved pain-free treatment in 84% of cases and intraosseous injection in 68%, both significantly superior to repeat IANB (32%) or PDL with 2% lidocaine (48%) after initial IANB failure (p=0.001) [[PMID:22414822]]. Kanaa et al. used 2.0 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine for the initial IANB and did not report the volume used for the supplemental buccal infiltration beyond the standard cartridge delivered.

10. **Reserve intrapulpal injection as last resort.** Eldafrawi et al. (2026) identify intrapulpal anesthesia as essential when all other techniques fail; deliver under back-pressure once access is established [[PMID:41808844]]. Eldafrawi et al. did not report a specific intrapulpal volume.

11. **Document all injections, volumes, agent concentrations, and HP-VAS scores** at each procedural stage for medicolegal records and to guide future visits.

### 4b. Decision Tree

**IF** patient presents with moderate-to-severe preoperative pain on HP-VAS (>54 mm) in a mandibular first molar with SIP
**THEN** plan COMB strategy: deliver 2-cartridge IANB and schedule PDL injection prophylactically before access preparation
**BECAUSE** preoperative pain level is the strongest independent predictor of intraoperative failure [[PMID:35947082]], and COMB strategy (RR=1.86) is statistically equivalent to SUPP while eliminating reactive decision-making mid-procedure [[PMID:40734367]]

**IF** lip numbness is absent at 15–20 minutes after IANB injection
**THEN** repeat IANB before proceeding; do not advance to pulpal testing or access
**BECAUSE** a missed block rate of 7.7% occurs with single-cartridge dosing; missed block is a deposition failure that must be corrected before supplemental techniques are attempted [[PMID:25770038]]

**IF** lip numbness is confirmed but patient reports pain >54 mm on HP-VAS during access preparation
**THEN** stop instrumentation; administer PDL injection of 0.6 mL of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine per root; allow 2–3 minutes; retest before continuing
**BECAUSE** PDL injection with 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine achieved 87.1% success when combined with preoperative aceclofenac 100 mg and 66.7% without, significantly outperforming 3% plain mepivacaine (p<0.05) [[PMID:40321815]]

**IF** PDL injection fails and patient remains >54 mm HP-VAS
**THEN** administer articaine buccal infiltration and/or intraosseous injection with 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine
**BECAUSE** articaine buccal infiltration achieved 84% and intraosseous injection 68% pain-free treatment — both significantly superior to repeat IANB (32%) or PDL with 2% lidocaine with epinephrine (48%) after initial IANB failure (p=0.001) [[PMID:22414822]]

**IF** patient scores high on MDAS (anxious adult) with SIP
**THEN** provide music distraction via patient-selected headphones throughout the entire appointment before initiating any injection
**BECAUSE** music distraction produced significantly higher IANB success than 0.5 mg etizolam or placebo in high-anxiety SIP patients (p<0.01) [[PMID:41360139]]

## Module 3 — Advanced Anesthesia Techniques and Newer Injectable Materials

**The literature is currently divided on this topic.** Two high-quality 2025 systematic reviews reach partially conflicting conclusions on which anesthetic agent optimizes IANB success in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP). De Carvalho et al. (2025, n=1262, 12 RCTs) found no significant efficacy difference among lidocaine 2%, articaine 4%, bupivacaine 0.5%, and mepivacaine 2% for IANB, concluding that lidocaine 2% with epinephrine is the most cost-effective (negative ICER values) [[PMID:41075922]]. Nagendrababu et al. (2019, n=750, 11 RCTs) found mepivacaine ranked highest by SUCRA (0.81), with statistically significant superiority over lidocaine (RR=1.42; 95% CI 1.04–1.95) [[PMID:30638269]]. The larger, more recent meta-analysis [[PMID:41075922]] is preferred; treat mepivacaine's ranking advantage as a signal worthy of note rather than practice-changing guidance. Current standard-of-care supports lidocaine 2% with 1:100,000 epinephrine as the first-line agent.

**Why IANB Alone Fails in Hot Teeth.** IANB success in SIP mandibular first molars is as low as 28%, and supplemental articaine buccal infiltration after failed IANB achieves only 42% success in first molars and 48% in second molars [[PMID:26831048]]. This underscores that no single injection reliably resolves the "hot tooth." Anatomically, radiographic data in 201 adults confirms the mandibular foramen lies approximately 3.88 mm above the occlusal plane and 19.35 mm from the anterior ramus border — high variation (range: −3.11 to +9.70 mm from the occlusal plane) explains many targeting failures [[PMID:40500816]].

**Supplemental Strategy: SUPP vs. COMB.** A 2025 network meta-analysis of 28 RCTs confirmed that both supplemental injection after IANB failure (SUPP; RR=2.02; SUCRA=85.1%) and combining supplemental injections with primary IANB (COMB; RR=1.86; SUCRA=64.9%) significantly outperform IANB alone, with no significant difference in effectiveness between SUPP and COMB [[PMID:40734367]]. The evidence quality ranged from low to high. Because SUPP and COMB perform equivalently, the choice between them may be guided by patient and operator factors; the evidence base does not specify a resource-efficiency argument for either approach.

**Intraligamentary (ILI) Supplemental Injection.** When primary IANB fails, intraligamentary injection of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine combined with pre-operative aceclofenac 100 mg achieves 87.1% success; without aceclofenac, the same articaine ILI achieves 66.7% [[PMID:40321815]]. Plain 3% mepivacaine ILI achieves 60.6% with aceclofenac and 53.1% without — these figures are drawn directly from Ghosh et al. (2025, n=126) [[PMID:40321815]]. Articaine ILI significantly outperformed mepivacaine ILI regardless of premedication (p<0.05) [[PMID:40321815]].

**Premedication: NSAIDs and Corticosteroids.** High-quality evidence (n=1,034; 13 RCTs; GRADE: high) confirms oral NSAID premedication 30–60 minutes before IANB nearly doubles success rates (RR=1.96; 95% CI 1.63–2.35) [[PMID:30903123]] [[PMID:29709297]]. Ibuprofen >400 mg is the threshold dose — ibuprofen ≤400 mg showed no statistically significant benefit (RR=1.78; 95% CI 0.90–3.55) [[PMID:29709297]]. A 2026 systematic review (8 RCTs; GRADE: moderate-to-high) confirms oral dexamethasone premedication significantly improves IANB success (RR=1.80; 95% CI 1.35–2.41), with both 0.5 mg and 4 mg doses effective, and Trial Sequential Analysis declaring evidence conclusive [[PMID:41981812]]. A network meta-analysis (n=1,654; 19 RCTs) found dexamethasone ranked first among premedications (RR=2.92 vs. placebo; SUCRA=0.96), with NSAIDs and tramadol also significantly superior to placebo [[PMID:29480930]]. Dexamethasone and NSAIDs showed equivalent head-to-head effectiveness [[PMID:41981812]].

**Adjuncts to Local Anesthetic Solution.** Adding 0.2 mL of 10% magnesium sulphate to replace an equivalent volume of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine produced mean perioperative pain of 0.16±0.37 vs. 3.13±0.77 in the lidocaine-only group (p=0.01, n=86) [[PMID:39213456]]. An earlier RCT (n=124) using 1% magnesium sulphate added to 1.8% lidocaine with 1:88,000 epinephrine achieved 82% IANB success vs. 53% in controls (p<0.001) [[PMID:31306506]]. However, the 2022 PROSPERO-registered systematic review (n=434, 14 RCTs; GRADE: very low to low) found that pooled additives to lidocaine — including sodium bicarbonate and mannitol — showed no significant effect on IANB success in SIP (RR=1.22; 95% CI 0.98–1.52; I²=0%) [[PMID:35398916]]. Magnesium sulphate data are promising but the individual RCTs are not yet captured within a high-quality SR specific to this additive; treat as provisional.

**Needle-Free Delivery and Solution Modification.** The needle-free Comfort-in™ system (delivering 4% articaine) achieved 71.0% anesthetic success comparable to conventional needle injection, with significantly lower injection pain (NRS 2.13±2.17 vs. 6.03±3.15) in a 62-patient RCT [[PMID:38459266]]. Preheating 2% lignocaine to 42°C (injected at 37°C) and buffering with 0.18 mL of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate both significantly reduced intraoperative pain versus room-temperature conventional lignocaine (p<0.001, n=252); preheated solution performed best, followed by buffered, then conventional [[PMID:36281366]].

**Modified Akinosi Technique (MAT).** In an 80-patient RCT of SIP mandibular molars, MAT using 2% lignocaine with 1:200,000 adrenaline produced no-pain outcomes in 80% of patients versus 51% with standard IANB [[PMID:40820098]]. MAT is particularly valuable when limited mouth opening prevents conventional IANB landmark identification.

**Ibuprofen SR as Buccal Infiltration Adjunct.** Ibuprofen sustained-release 800 mg taken 1 hour before 3.6 mL articaine buccal infiltration raised anesthetic success to 73.3% vs. 46.7% placebo (p<0.05, n=60) and also reduced postoperative pain at 6 and 48 hours [[PMID:38528685]].

### 4a. Procedural Protocol

1. **Premedicate 30–60 minutes before the appointment.** Prescribe ibuprofen >400 mg (600–800 mg is the dose range studied in the included RCTs) orally 30–60 minutes before the appointment; ibuprofen ≤400 mg confers no statistically significant benefit [[PMID:29709297]]. When NSAIDs are contraindicated, dexamethasone significantly improves IANB success at doses of 0.5 mg and 4 mg — the evidence base does not specify a preferred dose between these two [[PMID:41981812]].

2. **Target IANB injection site using radiographic landmarks.** Use the preoperative periapical or panoramic radiograph to estimate foramen height: target approximately 3.88 mm above the occlusal plane and 19.35 mm from the anterior ramus border [[PMID:40500816]]. Deposit 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine; the evidence base for this module does not specify an injection rate in seconds for this volume.

3. **Confirm lip numbness at 5 minutes.** If profound lip numbness is not achieved within 5 minutes, do not initiate access — reposition and reinject. Lip numbness is necessary but not sufficient for pulpal anesthesia in SIP.

4. **Consider MAT if mouth opening is restricted.** Deposit 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine with 1:200,000 adrenaline using the closed-mouth Akinosi approach; MAT achieved no-pain outcomes in 80% of SIP patients vs. 51% with standard IANB [[PMID:40820098]].

5. **Apply cryotherapy adjunct (optional).** Place an ice pack in the buccal vestibule over the target molar for 5 minutes immediately after IANB injection. This raised success rates from 40.8% to 59.2% and reduced supplemental ILI need from 67.1% to 50% in a 152-patient RCT [[PMID:37268843]]. Note: this RCT enrolled patients aged 10–17; no RCT in this evidence base evaluated cryotherapy exclusively in adults — treat as provisional for adult practice pending adult-specific data.

6. **Wait 15 minutes before commencing access.** Initiate access cavity preparation only after 15 minutes of elapsed time post-injection with lip numbness confirmed; Gandhi et al. used 15 minutes as the assessment interval in their n=252 RCT [[PMID:36281366]].

7. **At first sign of intraoperative pain, deliver ILI immediately.** Inject 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine intraligamentally. Ghosh et al. did not report a specific volume per site, but achieved 87.1% rescue success when combined with aceclofenac premedication [[PMID:40321815]].

8. **If ILI fails, deliver intrapulpal injection under back-pressure.** Eldafrawi et al. (narrative review) identify intrapulpal anesthesia as essential when all other techniques fail [[PMID:41808844]]; specific volume and concentration were not reported in the papers included in this evidence base — use whatever cartridge is available and inject under resistance.

9. **Document failure pattern for next visit.** If multiple supplemental injections were required, upgrade premedication and consider MAT or a combined SUPP strategy for the next appointment [[PMID:40734367]].

## Module 4 — Clinical Outcomes, Complications, and Evidence-Based Selection Criteria

**The central clinical problem this module addresses:** the mandibular molar with irreversible pulpitis (SIP) is the most anesthesia-resistant tooth in adult endodontic practice. Understanding why techniques succeed or fail — and what the evidence says about each rescue strategy — is what separates systematic clinical decision-making from trial and error.

**Why the inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) fails in SIP**

Inferior alveolar nerve blocks with 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine achieve profound pulpal anesthesia in only 69% of mandibular molar SIP cases when delivered as a conventional block [[PMID:39609974]]. The core mechanism is peripheral sensitization: inflamed pulpal tissue lowers the activation threshold of tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels, requiring higher local anesthetic concentrations at the nerve membrane than standard IANB volumes reliably provide. Radiographic landmark identification compounds failure: panoramic analysis shows the mandibular foramen sits a mean of 3.88 mm above the occlusal plane and 19.35 mm from the anterior ramus border, with individual variation spanning from −3.11 mm to +9.70 mm relative to the occlusal plane — meaning a "standard" injection height is anatomically incorrect in a significant proportion of patients [[PMID:40500816]].

**Technique selection by outcome data**

The Gow-Gates block, targeting the mandibular condyle neck and anesthetizing the entire third division at a single site, achieves 84% success in SIP mandibular molars versus 69% for conventional IANB — a statistically significant difference in this RCT of 102 patients [[PMID:39609974]]. When primary block techniques fail, supplemental intraligamentary (PDL) injections are the next tier. A double-blinded RCT (n=198) found that supplemental PDL injections of diclofenac sodium or ketorolac tromethamine reduced intraoperative VAS scores significantly more than supplemental PDL lidocaine (p<0.05) in both normotensive and hypertensive patients with SIP, with no adverse effects reported [[PMID:40705444]].

**Agent selection: the articaine versus lidocaine debate**

**The literature is currently divided on this topic.** The Cochrane review by St George et al. (2018, n=19,223) found low-quality evidence that 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine was superior to 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for posterior teeth with SIP requiring RCT (49% vs. 31% IANB success; RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.10–2.32) [[PMID:29990391]]. Conversely, a 2025 PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis (n=1,262) found no statistically significant difference in IANB efficacy among lidocaine 2%, articaine 4%, bupivacaine 0.5%, and mepivacaine 2% for mandibular molar SIP, and identified 2% lidocaine with epinephrine as the most cost-effective agent using incremental cost-effectiveness ratio analysis [[PMID:41075922]]. The larger and more recent meta-analysis favors lidocaine as the first-line cost-effective choice; however, the Cochrane review's articaine superiority finding for SIP specifically cannot be dismissed. Current standard of care: use 2% lidocaine 1:100,000 epinephrine as first-line; escalate to 4% articaine 1:100,000 epinephrine as primary or supplemental agent when initial IANB fails. **Note:** the Cochrane review includes a declared industry conflict (Dentsply); its articaine-superiority finding should be interpreted with this limitation in mind [[PMID:29990391]].

**Adjunctive pharmacological strategies**

Pre-treatment systemic dexamethasone increases IANB success rate versus placebo (OR=2.59; 95% CI 1.46–4.59) and reduces postoperative pain at 6 h (MD −1.43), 12 h (MD −1.65), and 24 h (MD −1.27) on VAS scales in patients with SIP [[PMID:35890176]]. No large double-blinded RCT with standardized dosing has yet isolated dexamethasone's contribution independent of technique variation across the included studies; the OR estimate should be treated as supporting evidence pending a dedicated high-quality RCT. Local tramadol increases anesthetic success versus placebo (OR=2.2; 95% CI 1.30–3.79) but also significantly increases adverse effects (OR=7.72; 95% CI 1.37–43.46) [[PMID:36292314]] — risk-benefit weighing is mandatory before use. Post-procedurally, combination paracetamol + NSAID therapy outperforms either agent alone for single-dose pain relief after endodontic procedures [[PMID:40755177]].

**Complications of supplemental techniques**

A case report documented focal mandibular osteonecrosis of the interdental septum between teeth 46 and 47, four months after intraosseous (IO) anesthesia, confirmed by CBCT as sequestrum with periapical osteolysis extending to the mandibular canal; the authors stated this was the first reported case in a healthy patient [[PMID:39854973]]. This recommendation rests on a single case report (Geusens 2025, n=1 tooth [[PMID:39854973]]) — no RCT or prospective cohort has evaluated IO osteonecrosis incidence; treat as a rare complication signal warranting technique precision, not a contraindication to IO use.
